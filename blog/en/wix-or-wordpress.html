Wix or WordPress? If you are building a website for your business in Spain, these two names come up sooner or later. Wix is an all-in-one website builder: the editor, the hosting and the upkeep come in a single subscription. WordPress is free, open-source software that you install with the web host of your choice, with more control and more upkeep on your side. The short answer: Wix suits you best if you want something easy and would rather not deal with the technical side; WordPress.org suits you if you value flexibility and have time for maintenance. In this Wix vs WordPress guide, we compare the two on design, features, SEO, speed, maintenance, languages, growth, switching and cost over time. We don't quote plan prices, because they change often: check them on the official websites.

Wix vs WordPress: what is the difference?

Wix is a closed platform: you build your website with its tools, and it hosts the site in exchange for a subscription. WordPress is free software that you install with a host you choose, and you can use it to create almost any kind of website. With Wix you gain speed and simplicity but depend on the platform; with WordPress you gain freedom and flexibility but take on more work.

Think of shop premises: Wix is like renting a unit that is already fitted out, where you can open straight away but can't move the walls; WordPress is like building your own, which you design as you like and can move, but the building work and the upkeep are yours.

Wix: an all-in-one website builder

Wix's dashboard brings the visual editor, design templates, hosting, the SSL certificate and platform updates together. You build your site with a drag-and-drop editor and pay a subscription for as long as it is published.

The trade-off is that you depend on the platform. Wix's own help centre explains that a Wix site has to stay on Wix's servers and can't be hosted with another provider. A developer can add custom code to a Wix site, but the site still lives only on Wix.

WordPress.org vs WordPress.com: two ways to use WordPress

WordPress.org is a content management system (CMS) released under the GPL licence. You download it for free, but to have a website you need hosting and a domain; then you install it and pick a theme (the look) and plugins (extra features). It gives you a lot of control, and in return asks for decisions: updates, backups and security are your job, or the job of whoever you hire.

Don't confuse it with WordPress.com, which hosts it for you: it looks after the server and the updates, although some features, such as installing your own plugins, are only available on some plans. In approach it is closer to Wix, with WordPress underneath and the option to export your content.

In short, you are choosing between three options:

  • Wix: a simple website you build yourself, with everything included in the subscription, but less independence.
  • WordPress.org: a lot of freedom and functionality, with your own hosting and the maintenance on your side.
  • WordPress.com: WordPress without managing a server, a middle way whose features depend on the plan.

Design: Wix templates vs WordPress themes

With Wix, you start from a template and build your pages in a visual editor; with WordPress, you pick a theme and can customise almost everything, as far as your skills or your budget allow. Wix makes good design easy, with plenty of options inside each template; WordPress gives you more design freedom.

Whatever the tools, good design helps people find what they came for quickly: what you offer, where you are based and how to contact you. Before you publish, check the user experience on a phone:

  • text that is easy to read without zooming in;
  • buttons that are easy to tap, starting with the phone, WhatsApp and email ones;
  • short forms that are quick to fill in on a small screen;
  • contact details users can find without scrolling far.

Designing with Wix: templates and a drag-and-drop editor

Wix offers templates for many kinds of businesses and a drag-and-drop editor with simple design tools: building a page means placing elements such as text, images and buttons where you want and seeing the result straight away. Colours and fonts change in a few clicks, so users with no design experience can still get a professional look.

That freedom has limitations. The design is limited to what Wix's editor allows, and Wix doesn't let you switch an existing site to a different template: to use another one, you create a new site and move your content across. So choose the right template carefully at the start.

Designing with WordPress: themes and the block editor

In WordPress you pick one of many themes, free or premium, which sets the design and structure of the site, then customise it with the theme settings and the block editor. You can switch theme later and keep your posts and pages, although menus and layouts may need a bit of adjusting.

With some code, or a developer's help, you can go much further, right up to a fully custom web design. That flexibility calls for judgement: a bloated theme, or one its author no longer maintains, can slow your site down and cause compatibility problems.

Features: Wix apps vs WordPress plugins

Both platforms let you add functionality, but in different ways: Wix offers apps built into its dashboard, while WordPress users rely on plugins, many of them made by independent developers. Before building anything, start from exactly what visitors will do on your site: get in touch, book an appointment, buy something or simply look up information.

Wix includes its own basic features plus an App Market with apps for forms, bookings, live chat, a blog, a store and marketing tools, for example email campaigns. You add them from the dashboard and they fit in with the rest of the site. The advantage is fewer loose parts and less risk of conflicts; the drawback is that you are limited to what is available on Wix's platform.

A plugin is an extension that adds a function to WordPress: forms, SEO, backups, caching, security, languages, email marketing, ecommerce or custom functionality. The official directory holds a lot of free plugins, and developers sell premium ones, so you can create almost any feature. Installing a plugin is easy, but each one is another part to update and keep an eye on, so check that its developer still maintains it. If you take appointments, our guide to choosing a booking system explains what to look for.

Selling online: Wix ecommerce vs WooCommerce

To sell online, Wix's ecommerce features are built into its dashboard: you create your catalogue, add products and manage payments and orders in one place, on one of its paid plans that accept online payments.

In WordPress, a shop often means WooCommerce, a free, open-source ecommerce plugin with extensions, some of them paid, for things like payment options and shipping. It gives you a lot of flexibility and suits custom ecommerce projects, but it needs suitable hosting and more maintenance. For a small range of products, built-in ecommerce saves effort; WooCommerce is better suited to a catalogue that is going to grow.

Blogging and content

When it comes to blogging, WordPress started life as a publishing tool, and it shows: categories, tags and custom page addresses make it great for running a blog with many posts. Wix also has a built-in blog, which is good enough for a small business blog. If content sits at the heart of your marketing, WordPress is better suited to a large blog; and if writing isn't your thing, read how to write website content when you're not a writer.

Ease of use: do you need technical skills?

Not for Wix, a little for WordPress.com and a lot more for WordPress.org.

Wix's drag-and-drop editor is designed for users with no experience: you pick a template, build your pages by changing the text and photos, and publish. That ease of use is its great strength, and it makes it easy to get started even if you have never built a website. The more you customise the design, though, the more hours you'll spend fine-tuning it.

WordPress can also be used without writing code, thanks to its block editor, but building a site with it asks for more decisions: hosting, theme, plugins, plus security and SEO settings. Many hosting companies offer tools that install WordPress in a few clicks; the rest is a matter of setting things up and learning a bit. Nothing impossible, but it takes time, curiosity or a budget to hand it over. With little experience, Wix is the better starting point; with some practice, WordPress offers more flexibility.

Let's be clear about what each option asks of you:

  • Wix: a few hours to build a basic site, then the habit of keeping your text up to date.
  • WordPress.com: a bit more learning, but no server to manage.
  • WordPress.org: learning how to build and set up the site, then regular maintenance, or someone you pay to do it.

Wix vs WordPress for SEO: which helps you rank better on Google?

When it comes to SEO (search engine optimisation), both let you handle the basics: page titles, meta descriptions, page addresses, content and speed. WordPress gives you more technical control and Wix more simplicity, but neither ranks your website on its own.

What counts is useful content, a clear page for each service you offer and each area you cover, a website that loads quickly on mobile, and details that match your Google Business Profile, which appears in local search. If, like many small businesses, you serve customers in your area, start with what local SEO is, then how to rank a local business website on Google and how to find the right local keywords; once your site is live, track your results with a few basic website metrics.

SEO tools in Wix

Wix comes with built-in SEO and marketing tools, including email marketing. You can edit the title and meta description of each page, page addresses, redirects, structured data and the sitemap, and connect your site to Google Search Console, one of Google's free tools, to see which searches bring users to your site. For a freelancer's or small business website, these tools cover the basic needs; the limit is that you have no access to the server.

SEO in WordPress

WordPress lets you choose the structure of your page addresses, install SEO plugins and decide on the hosting and caching. That is an advantage if you publish content often and want to fine-tune how each page appears in search. But an SEO plugin you never configure improves nothing: it needs judgement and regular attention.

Speed: Wix vs WordPress, which loads faster?

Speed is important, but neither platform is fast or slow by definition: your site's speed depends on what you put on it and, with WordPress, on your hosting too.

On Wix, you don't pick a server or touch the code, so your levers are the content you create: light images, only the apps and features you need, and moderate animations. On WordPress.org, four things come into play: the host, the theme, the plugins and the images. A good host with caching and a lightweight theme help; a heavy theme loaded with plugins slows the site down.

Whatever you use, the same habits help:

  • Compress and resize images before uploading them.
  • Install only the apps or plugins you actually use.
  • Keep the design of your home page light, without heavy animations or videos.
  • Test your website on a phone, the way many users will see it, with Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool.

Speed affects how users experience your website and how it ranks in search results. We go into detail in how load time shapes your Google ranking.

Maintenance and security: who looks after your website?

When it comes to maintenance, Wix looks after the platform; with WordPress.org, you do, or whoever you hire; WordPress.com manages the server for you.

If you use Wix, it manages the servers behind your website, keeps everything up to date and handles the SSL certificate. Your side is your account: strong passwords, two-step verification and control over who has access. Here is who handles what with each option:

What WordPress.org leaves to you

The core software, the theme and the plugins all need to be kept up to date. The official WordPress documentation recommends always updating to the latest version and backing up your site before you start. Minor and security releases of the core can install automatically; major versions, themes and plugins, including WooCommerce if you run a shop, need your attention. A basic maintenance checklist:

  • Update the core, your theme and your plugins.
  • Set up automatic backups of your files and database, and check that you can restore them.
  • Delete plugins and themes you no longer use.
  • Use strong passwords and two-step verification for every admin user.

WordPress.com handles the server and core updates for you, which lightens this load. Your content and your logins remain your responsibility, whatever the plan.

Legal pages and data protection: your job on any platform

Whatever the platform, your website collects data through its forms, and in Spain it needs a legal notice (aviso legal), a privacy policy and a cookie policy. Handling that data is your responsibility: you have to write those texts, explain what information you collect and why, and keep them up to date, or entrust them to someone who knows how.

Multilingual websites: Spanish, English and Valencian

Wix has built-in tools for multilingual sites, so you manage every language from the same editor; in WordPress, you add languages with a plugin, which is one more part to maintain. Either way, it is important to plan your languages from the start.

If you work in Valencia, Alicante or Elche, your customers may search, for example, in Spanish, Valencian or English, and each language involves creating and updating a set of pages. Before you create other language versions:

  • decide which languages you really need, based on who your customers are;
  • give each language its own pages and addresses, so search engines can show the right version to each user;
  • translate the essentials with care: services, area, contact details and legal pages;
  • update every version when you change something.

Growth: what happens when your website gets bigger?

Wix grows as far as the platform allows; WordPress.org can grow a great deal further, without the limits of a closed platform, although a bigger site requires more server resources and maintenance.

  • With Wix, you add pages and apps from Wix's App Market as you need them. The ceiling is the platform: if one day you need something it doesn't allow, you will have to adapt or rebuild the site with other tools.
  • With WordPress.org, you can add languages, a shop, custom functionality or a blog with a long archive of posts without asking a platform's permission, as long as your hosting grows with your needs and someone handles the extra maintenance.
  • With WordPress.com, the plan decides which features and design options you get; if you outgrow it, you can move up a plan or take your content to a host of your own.

Switching platforms: can you leave later?

With WordPress, yes: your content, files and database can move to another host. With Wix, you will have to rebuild the website elsewhere, because a Wix site can't be hosted outside Wix.

If you use Wix and want to leave, your text, photos and data are yours, but you have to create the site and its design again with other tools. Your domain is another matter: you can connect it to another website or transfer it to another provider, so it is better to register it in your own name. Here is our advice on choosing a domain name.

WordPress makes moving relatively easy. Its built-in export tool, with no plugin needed, gives you a file of your content that you can import into another WordPress site, and if you host it yourself, your files and database can be copied to any compatible host. The same export is available on WordPress.com.

No choice leaves you completely independent, so a few precautions are important:

  • Register your domain in your own name, and keep your login details and the email address linked to your account safe.
  • Keep copies of your original text, photos and logo: they help if you ever rebuild.
  • Decide who has admin access and write it down.
  • Know what happens to your website if you stop paying the subscription or your host.

Cost over time: what do you actually pay for?

With Wix you pay a subscription that includes hosting; with WordPress.org you pay for hosting and a domain, and your own time weighs more. We don't copy plan prices here, because they change often: check them on the official websites, look at all the options, pick the plan that fits you best and compare the renewal price, not just the first-year offer.

With Wix, you pay for:

  • a subscription for as long as the site is published, which comes with hosting, the SSL certificate and platform maintenance;
  • a paid plan to connect your own domain and remove Wix ads, even for a basic site;
  • some apps and extra features, which are charged separately;
  • a plan that accepts payments if you want to sell.

The free plan is good for trying the tools, but too limited to present a company: your web address is a Wix one and visitors see Wix ads.

With WordPress, you pay for:

  • hosting and a domain name, renewed for as long as the site exists;
  • premium themes or plugins if you want them, although you can start with free ones;
  • the fees of a developer or technician if you won't handle maintenance or custom changes yourself;
  • with WordPress.com, a subscription instead of hosting, with features that depend on the plan.

The cost that never appears on an invoice: your time

Comparisons often forget your time, yet it is an important cost. Count the hours you'll invest: learning, building the site, writing the text, updating everything and fixing the unexpected. As a freelancer, those are hours you don't spend with clients. Before you decide, answer four questions:

  • How many hours a month can you give your website?
  • Who will update it, and who will fix it if something breaks?
  • Do you need an online shop with products and payments from day one?
  • What happens if you change platform in a few years?

Wix or WordPress: which suits your business best?

There is no single winner between the two platforms: both are valid options for small business websites, and neither is better for everyone. The right choice depends on how many hours you can give, the freedom you need and the kind of website you want to build.

Choose Wix if:

  • you want a quick, easy way to build a simple website yourself, without any programming;
  • your site will be a basic showcase, with a few pages, a simple design and a contact form;
  • you prefer paying a subscription and forgetting about server maintenance;
  • the features you need are available in Wix's App Market;
  • you don't plan to change platform later.

Choose WordPress.org if:

  • you need full control over the design, the features and the SEO;
  • you'll post on your blog regularly or build an online shop with WooCommerce;
  • you want to be able to change host without rebuilding the site;
  • you have some experience with websites, time to maintain one, or a budget to hand it over.

WordPress.com sits in between: it is a good fit if you want WordPress but would rather skip server management, and you accept that some features depend on your plan.

Three example situations in Spain

These are made-up examples to help you picture it, not client stories:

  • An electrician in Elda who just needs to be found and called: a showcase website with his services, the area he covers, his phone number and a form. If he wants to build it himself, Wix fits best.
  • An accountancy firm in Alicante that likes to post articles regularly, in Spanish and English: WordPress.org gives it more room, provided someone handles the maintenance.
  • A ceramicist in Elche with a growing catalogue: Wix's store and WooCommerce both work, depending on how many hours she has and the flexibility she wants.

Frequently asked questions

Is Wix or WordPress better for a small business?
It depends on how much control you want and how many hours you can give your website. Wix suits a simple site that you want to build quickly without maintenance work; WordPress.org suits a business that wants freedom, an active blog or a custom shop, and has the hours or the budget to maintain it. If you would rather handle neither, you can have the website built for you.
WordPress.org vs WordPress.com: what is the difference?
WordPress.org is the free software you download and install with a host you pay for, with full control, more flexibility and the maintenance on your side. WordPress.com is a hosted service: it manages the server for you, although some features, such as installing your own plugins, depend on the plan. They are two different products built on the same software.
Is Wix free?
Wix has a free plan for trying the tool, with Wix ads and a Wix web address. To connect your own domain and remove the ads, you need one of its paid plans. Prices change, so check them on the official Wix website.
Is WordPress really free?
The WordPress.org software is free and open source, under the GPL licence. What you pay for is hosting, a domain and, if you want them, premium themes or plugins, plus the hours spent on maintenance or a technician's help. WordPress.com works by subscription and also has a free plan with limitations.
Can I move a Wix site to WordPress?
Not directly. Wix states that its sites only run on its own servers and can't be hosted elsewhere. Your content is yours, but the site has to be rebuilt in WordPress and the text and photos moved across; your domain can then be connected to the new site.
Is Wix or WordPress better for SEO?
Both let you handle basic SEO: page titles, meta descriptions, page addresses, content and speed. WordPress offers more technical control and Wix more simplicity, but neither ranks a website on its own. What counts is useful content and clear pages about your services and the area you cover.
Which is better for an ecommerce site, Wix or WooCommerce?
Wix builds ecommerce into its dashboard, with fewer parts to maintain. WooCommerce, the ecommerce plugin for WordPress, offers more flexibility but needs suitable hosting, more decisions and more maintenance. For a small, simple catalogue, built-in ecommerce is enough; for a custom ecommerce project, WooCommerce gives you more room.
Do I need to know how to code to use WordPress?
No. WordPress is managed through a block editor, without code. But installing it with a host, choosing plugins and handling updates and backups call for some experience, or help from a professional.
