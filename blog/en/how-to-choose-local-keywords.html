If you're a freelancer or run a small business in Spain, getting found on Google starts with local keyword research, the foundation of local SEO. A great website won't bring you work if nobody can find it when they search for ‘plumber in Elda’, ‘dentist in Alicante’ or, in Spanish, ‘fontanero urgente Alicante’. This guide explains how to find local SEO keywords with free tools, choose the ones worth targeting and use them on your website and your Google Business Profile.

What is local keyword research?

Local keyword research is the process of finding the words people type into Google when they need products or services in a specific location, then choosing which of those search terms your website should target.

It's the first step of any local SEO strategy: the keywords you choose shape your pages, your content and your Google Business Profile.

Examples of local keywords

A local keyword combines what you offer with a location, or with a sign of local intent such as ‘near me’. For example:

  • ‘electrician in Valencia’: a trade and a city;
  • ‘emergency plumber Alicante’: a trade, an urgency and a city;
  • ‘hairdresser near me’: a trade and proximity;
  • ‘how much does a bathroom renovation cost in Elda’: a question asked before hiring;
  • ‘fontanero urgente Alicante’: the second example, searched in Spanish.

How it differs from general SEO keyword research

General keyword research looks for the terms with the largest search volume across a whole country.

In keyword research for local businesses, small numbers are normal: a handful of monthly searches from potential customers nearby can be worth more than thousands of visits from people searching far from where you work.

What counts is how relevant a search is to your business, not its size.

Why keyword research matters for local SEO

Google doesn't show the same results to all users. For a search with local intent, it takes location into account, and the search results often include a map with nearby businesses, the Google Map Pack, above the usual list of websites.

The right SEO keywords help you appear in those local search results.

Google's local ranking factors

Google explains that local results depend mainly on three factors:

  • Relevance: how well a business matches what someone is searching for.
  • Distance: how far the business is from the location in the search or, if no place is typed, from the user.
  • Prominence: how well known the business is, based on information such as reviews and links.

In local SEO, keywords work on the first ranking factor: they help Google understand what you do and where, so your pages are relevant to the right local search queries.

What local keyword research changes on your website

Your keyword list also helps you decide which pages to create and what content to put on them.

Instead of guessing, you write content for the search terms your potential customers really use, which can improve your SEO rankings and bring more relevant visitors to your business.

Types of local keywords, with examples

Local SEO keywords come in a few recurring types. Knowing each type, you can find missing keywords in your list.

Explicit and implicit local keywords

An explicit local keyword names the location, as in ‘locksmith Elche’.

An implicit one doesn't, but the local intent is still there: someone in Elche searching for ‘locksmith’ wants one nearby, and Google shows local businesses based on where the user is, since distance is a ranking factor.

Your site's content and your Google Business Profile should say clearly where you work, so Google can match you to queries with local intent; that's why you rarely need to write ‘near me’ on your pages.

Short-tail and long-tail keywords

Short-tail keywords are brief and broad, like ‘plumber Valencia’.

Long-tail keywords are longer and more specific, for example ‘water heater installation in Benimaclet’: they have less volume, but the person typing them knows what they want, and there's often less competition, which suits small local businesses.

A sensible local SEO strategy combines both, with one primary term for each page and several long-tail keywords to support it in your content.

Spanish, English and place-name variants

In Spain, many potential customers will be searching in Spanish even if you work in English, while expats may search in English. ‘Emergency plumber Alicante’ and ‘fontanero urgente Alicante’ are different queries, with their own volume and their own local competitors.

Some potential clients also add a language, for example ‘English-speaking dentist Alicante’.

Place names vary too: Alicante and Alacant, Elche and Elx, Valencia and València are the same places, and some users search with the Valencian name.

Research keywords for each variant, keep a separate keyword list for each language, and create content only in the languages you can serve customers in.

What is search intent and why does it matter?

Search intent is the goal behind a search: the information someone wants or the action they want to take.

It matters for SEO because Google tries to show, in its search results, the pages that best meet that goal, so a keyword only works if your content gives people what they're looking for.

There are four types of search intent:

  • Informational: the person wants to learn something, as in ‘how to unblock a sink’.
  • Commercial: they're comparing options, as in ‘hairdressers in Elche with good reviews’.
  • Transactional: they want to hire or buy now, as in ‘emergency plumber Elda’.
  • Navigational: they're looking for a specific business by name.

Match each search intent with the right page

Someone with transactional intent needs a service page that explains what you do, where you work and how to contact you.

Someone looking for information needs an answer first, and useful content such as an article can give it, with a link to your services.

Send an informational keyword to a sales page, or the reverse, and it will struggle to rank in the results.

Check the SERP before you choose a keyword

The quickest way to find the search intent behind a keyword is to look at the search results for it, the search engine results page (SERP). Local SERPs can include:

  • A map with local businesses: the query has local intent, so your Google Business Profile matters as much as your site.
  • Service pages from local companies: people want to hire.
  • Articles and guides: people want information, so educational content fits better.
  • Directories and large platforms: competition is strong, and a more specific target keyword may be easier to rank for.

Perform this SERP analysis for each keyword in a private window, and remember that local SERPs vary with location: the SERP you see in Valencia can be different from the one users see in Elche.

For some searches, Google also shows an AI Overview, a generated summary at the top of the SERP.

The points this AI summary covers are another clue to what users want to learn, and to the content your page should include.

How to do local keyword research in seven steps

You don't need paid SEO software to start local keyword research.

Follow these steps in order to build a realistic keyword list; how long it takes depends on how many services and areas you cover.

Step one: list your services and areas

Write down every service or product your business offers and every town or neighbourhood where you really work, and include the words your customers use. These are your seed keywords, the starting point to find more.

Be specific: ‘boiler installation’ says more than ‘plumbing’. For example, for a plumber based in Elda:

  • Services: leak repairs, boiler installation, bathroom renovations, unblocking drains.
  • Locations: Elda, Petrer and Monóvar.
  • Words customers use: ‘burst pipe’, ‘no hot water’, ‘fontanero’.

Combine each service with each location and you have your first keyword ideas: ‘leak repair Elda’, ‘boiler installation Petrer’, ‘fontanero Elda’.

A local shop can do the same with its key products, as in ‘electric bikes Alicante’ or ‘children's shoes Elche’.

Step two: collect the words your customers use

Your local clients already tell you how they search: their words come up in calls, WhatsApp messages, emails and quote requests.

To find them, think about the exact terms they use, and you'll discover phrases you might not use yourself.

Read your reviews and those of your local competitors too: they're a great source of the words people use.

Clients often describe a job in different words from yours: they type ‘no hot water’ where you'd say ‘water heater replacement’. Both belong on your list.

Step three: find more keywords with Google

Google itself is a free keyword research tool. Open a private window, type your service and your location, and look at:

  • Autocomplete: the suggestions that appear as you type, based on real searches.
  • People also ask: related questions, useful for your FAQ and your content.
  • Related searches: more variations, at the bottom of the results.

Repeat the exercise for every town you cover, in Spanish as well as English, and add every relevant keyword you find to your list, even if it looks small.

Step four: check search volume and seasonality

In SEO, search volume is the estimated number of monthly searches for a keyword.

Google Keyword Planner, a free tool inside Google Ads, gives you these estimates: you need a Google Ads account, but you don't have to spend anything. Its ‘Discover new keywords’ option helps you find related keywords, and you can set the location and language.

Keep two cautions in mind:

  • the figures are estimates, often shown as broad ranges, so use them to compare keywords rather than as exact counts;
  • local search volume for a small town can even look empty without meaning that nobody searches: the tool may simply have little data at that level.

Google Trends compares the relative popularity of terms over time and by region, without exact search numbers.

You can explore, for example, whether interest in ‘air conditioning repair’ rises in summer in the Valencian Community.

Step five: competitor analysis

Search for each important keyword and examine the businesses and platforms that appear in the local search results, both in the map and in the first organic results.

This SEO competitor analysis isn't about copying them: the aim is to discover what earns them their rankings and find gaps you can fill. Look at:

  • the keywords that competitors use in the titles and headings of their service pages;
  • the content of those pages, and the specific services and towns they mention or leave out;
  • their Google Business Profile categories and reviews.

If, for example, all your competitors target ‘plumber Alicante’ but none has a clear page for ‘boiler repair Playa de San Juan’, that gap may be your opportunity.

Step six: choose and prioritise your keywords

A long list isn't an SEO plan. Target the keywords that pass these four filters first:

  • Relevance: it describes something you really offer.
  • Location: it names or implies a town, city or area where you work.
  • Search intent: the person wants to hire, buy or visit, or needs information you can give.
  • Realistic competition: the results aren't dominated by large platforms or competitors you can't match yet.

Then think about business value: a keyword linked to the most profitable part of your business deserves priority, even with a smaller search volume.

Step seven: map each keyword to a page

Go through your list and give every page one target keyword and a few related variations in its content, and make sure no two pages compete for the same search.

For the plumber in Elda, the keyword map could look like this:

  • Home page: ‘plumber in Elda’.
  • Leak repairs: ‘leak repair Elda’ and ‘burst pipe repair Elda’.
  • Boilers: ‘boiler installation Elda’ and ‘water heater repair Petrer’.
  • Blog article: ‘how much does a bathroom renovation cost in Elda’.

Keep this SEO keyword map in a simple spreadsheet with each keyword, its search intent, its URL and the date you last updated the page: it will save you time later.

Local keyword research tools: free and paid

You can do solid keyword research for local SEO with free tools.

Paid SEO software saves time and adds data, but many local businesses can do without it at first.

Free tools from Google

  • Google Search: autocomplete, suggested questions and related searches.
  • Keyword Planner: keyword ideas and search volume estimates by location.
  • Google Trends: how interest in a term changes over time and by region.
  • Search Console: the queries for which your site already appears, with impressions, clicks and average position.
  • Google Business Profile: the local search terms people used to find your profile.

Keyword Planner needs a Google Ads account. If you've never used Google Ads, you'll learn how the account works in our Google Ads guide for beginners.

Google Search Console: your own search data

Search Console, Google's free SEO tool, shows the actual queries for which your site appears. It helps you discover local queries where you already rank without standing out: a good place to start if you want to improve your rankings.

AnswerThePublic

AnswerThePublic helps you discover the questions and phrases people search around a topic, which is useful when you're looking for content ideas. Its free use is limited.

Paid SEO tools

SEO tools such as Semrush, Mangools (KWFinder), Ahrefs or Moz add search volume estimates, a difficulty score for each target keyword and data on the rankings of competing pages.

A paid keyword research tool can be worth it once local SEO becomes an important source of work; until then, Google's own tools cover the essentials.

AI assistants and AI Overviews

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude can help you find keyword ideas: variations of your services, typical local queries or neighbourhoods around your base.

Treat the answers as ideas, not data: AI tools have no reliable figures on how many people search for each term, and AI answers can include phrases nobody uses.

Check that each idea is relevant before adding it to your list (see our selection of AI tools for freelancers).

As for AI Overviews, the basics of local SEO stay the same: clear, accurate content that answers a local question is useful to readers, whatever form the search results take.

Where to use your local keywords

For good SEO, use your keywords where they help readers and Google understand the content of each page. Natural wording matters more than repeating keywords.

On each page of your website

These on-page SEO elements are the most important:

  • the page title, which should include the target keyword and the location, and often appears as the clickable headline in Google;
  • the main heading (H1), with the keyword, and one or two subheadings;
  • the first paragraph, where the keyword fits naturally;
  • a short, readable URL, such as /boiler-installation-elda, which users see before they click;
  • the alt text of your photos, when it describes the image honestly;
  • your name, address and phone number (NAP), exactly as on your Google Business Profile.

The meta description doesn't directly affect your SEO ranking, but a clear one helps potential local customers decide to click on your result.

Service pages and location pages

Create a service page for each important service, built around its own target keyword.

If you work in several towns, you can create location pages too, each with its own URL, but only for places where you really work and only with specific content for each one: the jobs you do there and the neighbourhoods you cover.

Pages that repeat the same content and only change the city name add little for readers or for your SEO.

Google's spam policies describe pages created to rank for similar searches, such as near-identical pages for different cities, as doorway pages.

On your Google Business Profile

For local businesses, the Google Business Profile is often the first thing people find in local search, especially among the map listings, which makes it a key part of local SEO.

Use your keyword list to complete and improve it:

  • choose the primary category that describes your business most accurately, and add secondary categories only when they're relevant;
  • include your specific services or products, described in your customers' words;
  • write a description with clear information about what you do and in which area;
  • keep your name, address and phone number identical to those on your site.

Use your real business name, without adding keywords: Google's guidelines don't allow it, and breaking them can get your profile suspended.

In your blog content

Create content, such as articles and FAQ entries, for your informational keywords. An honest article targeting ‘how much does a bathroom renovation cost in Elda’, linked to your renovations section, can bring visitors who will need you later. Each useful article is also one more page that can rank in search results.

If writing isn't your thing, read how to write website content when you're not a writer.

Mistakes to avoid when choosing local keywords

These mistakes can undermine a local SEO strategy, but they're easy to avoid:

  • Keywords that are too generic: ‘plumber’ or ‘restaurant’ without a place bring competitors you can't beat and visitors you can't serve.
  • Cities where you don't work: they attract people your business can't help and confuse those who could hire you.
  • Keyword stuffing: repeating the same phrase makes content harder to read, and Google treats keyword stuffing as spam, which can hurt your SEO.
  • Ignoring searches in Spanish: if you work with locals, part of your market may never search in English.
  • Ignoring search intent: content that doesn't match what people want struggles to rank.
  • Never measuring: without data, you can't tell which keywords improve your rankings.

What is keyword cannibalisation?

Keyword cannibalisation happens when two of your pages target the same search and Google can't tell which one to show.

The fix: choose one main keyword per page, and merge or differentiate pages that cover the same topic.

How do you know if your local keywords are working?

Look at the local search queries that bring you impressions and clicks in Google Search Console, and the calls and clicks recorded in your Google Business Profile. If the right searches start to bring enquiries, your keywords are working.

  • In Search Console, see which pages perform well, and check impressions, clicks and rankings (average position) for each page's target keyword.
  • If a page appears for a query but few people click, improve its title and description.
  • In your Google Business Profile, check calls, direction requests, website clicks and local search terms.
  • If visitors arrive but don't get in touch, review the content and the contact options on that page.

How often to update your keyword list

There's no fixed calendar for reviewing your SEO keywords: update your list every few months and whenever you add a service or an area. Local SEO takes time and rankings move slowly, so give each change a few weeks before you judge it. For the basics, see how to know if your website works.

Your local keyword research checklist

  1. List what you offer and the areas you cover.
  2. Collect the words your local customers use, in Spanish and English.
  3. Expand your list with autocomplete, People also ask and related searches.
  4. Check search volume in Keyword Planner and trends in Google Trends.
  5. Look at your competitors and the SERP for each keyword.
  6. Create a keyword map with one target keyword per page.
  7. Use your keywords in titles, headings, text and your Google Business Profile.
  8. Measure your SEO results, then adjust.

Frequently asked questions

What is local keyword research?
Local keyword research is the process of finding the searches people make when they need products or services in a specific location, such as ‘electrician in Valencia’ or ‘emergency plumber Alicante’, and choosing which ones your website should target. It's the starting point of local SEO for freelancers and small businesses.
How many local keywords does my business need?
There's no fixed number: it depends on how many services you offer and how many areas you cover. Give each page one main keyword and a few related variations, start with your most important services, then expand your content gradually.
What's the difference between local keywords and other SEO keywords?
Other SEO keywords target searches without a place, often across a whole country, such as ‘how to fix a leaking tap’. Local keywords add a location or a local intent, such as ‘plumber in Elche’ or ‘plumber near me’, to reach customers in the areas you serve.
Which free tools can I use for local keyword research?
Google offers several ways to find keyword ideas: autocomplete, the People also ask box, related searches, Google Trends and Search Console, which shows the search terms where your site already appears. Google Keyword Planner, which estimates search volume, is free too, but you need a Google Ads account; you don't have to spend anything. AnswerThePublic has a limited free version.
Can AI help with local keyword research?
Yes, for ideas. An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude can suggest variations of your services, customer queries and areas around you, but AI tools have no reliable figures on how often people search for these terms. Check those keyword suggestions with Google Keyword Planner, Google Trends or Search Console before using them in your content.
Should I research keywords in English or Spanish?
Research the languages your customers use. Many locals in Spain search in Spanish, for example ‘fontanero urgente Alicante’, while expats may search for ‘emergency plumber Alicante’. Keep a separate keyword list for each language.
Do I need to add ‘near me’ to my website?
No. ‘Near me’ searches have local intent: Google uses the location of the person searching, so the phrase doesn't need to appear on your pages. What matters is that your site and your Google Business Profile say clearly what you do and which areas you serve, so local customers can find you.
How often should I review my local keywords?
There's no fixed rule: review your keywords every few months, and whenever you add a service or start working in a new area. Search Console shows which queries already bring you impressions, so you can adjust your pages to real data.
Should I mention towns where I don't work?
No. You could attract people you can't serve and confuse those who could hire you. Mention only the areas where you really work, and create a location page for each one only when you have something specific to say about it.
