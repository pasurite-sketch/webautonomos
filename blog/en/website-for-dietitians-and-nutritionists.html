Many people look up a nutritionist online before they book a first visit. Before they trust you with their diet, potential clients want to know who you are, what qualification you hold and how you work. Good nutritionist website design answers those questions in a few seconds; if your site leaves them in doubt, they may well choose someone else. This guide explains what your website needs, how to describe and design your services, how to present online sessions and which rules apply to dietitians and nutritionists in Spain.

Why does a nutritionist need a website?

A nutritionist needs a website because it is the one place where potential clients can check your qualification, understand how you work and get in touch, without depending on anyone else's platform. In nutrition, many people choose a professional they feel they can trust, and your site is where they get to know you.

Social media lets people discover you, but it depends on an algorithm and on rules set by someone else. Your site doesn't: you decide what visitors see, you explain what your practice offers and anyone can write to you at any time. Your social media profiles can send traffic to your site, and your site points back to them, so together they build your online presence.

What potential clients want to know first

Someone looking for a dietitian or a nutritionist usually wants to find out:

  • what you studied in nutrition and dietetics, and whether you are a member of a colegio (the professional association);
  • which nutrition services you offer and what the first session involves;
  • whether you see patients in person, by video call or both;
  • where you work, your hours and how to get in touch.

Qualification and colegiado number: the basis of your credibility

In Spain, dietitian-nutritionist (dietista-nutricionista) is a regulated health profession. Your credibility depends on what visitors can check for themselves: your degree, your colegio membership and your experience.

Your degree and your colegiado number

Show your university degree, for example the Grado en Nutrición Humana y Dietética, and, if you are a member of a colegio, your colegiado number. Spain's law on information society services (LSSI) asks anyone in a regulated profession to show certain information on their website, permanently and in an easy, direct and free way, including:

  • the colegio they belong to, if any, and their colegiado number;
  • their official academic qualification and, for a qualification obtained abroad, the country that issued it and its recognition in Spain;
  • the professional rules that apply to their work and how to access them.

If your practice is registered as a health centre with your regional health department, show its registration number too. All of this can help patients tell a qualified dietitian-nutritionist apart from someone who gives diet advice without a recognised qualification.

Patient testimonials: why to leave them out

Many website guides written for other countries suggest testimonials and client success stories. In Spain, health-advertising rules don't allow them: you can't use what patients, real or supposed, say about you to attract clients, and the same applies to famous people and other health professionals. Build trust with what anyone can verify instead: your qualification, your colegiado number, your approach and practical details about your sessions.

What should a dietitian website include?

A dietitian website needs a few essential pages: a homepage, an about page, a services page, a way to get in touch and the legal texts; a page on online sessions, a blog and answers to frequent questions are useful additions. Small practices don't need dozens of pages: keep the structure simple, so that visitors find each page in one or two clicks.

Homepage

This is where many visitors arrive first. In the first screen, give the key information: who you are, where you work, what you offer and how to make an appointment, with a clear button to call or write to you. Further down, include a short summary of your services and your qualification, with links to the sections where patients can learn more.

About page

The about page is where potential clients decide whether to trust you. Include your degree, your colegiado number, your training, your experience and a recent photo, then explain how you approach nutrition and what makes your way of working different. Write the content for patients, not for other professionals, and describe your expertise in plain words, so that clients learn how you work. A short introductory video, which you can create with your phone, also helps. Let your personality come through, as it is part of your personal brand; our guide to building your personal brand can help you put your career into words.

Services page

This page lists each service with what it involves, how long a session lasts, whether it takes place in person or online and, if you publish it, its full price. If you offer several services, create a short section for each. Patients often read it before deciding to get in touch, so keep each description short, specific and focused on the key facts.

Contact page

Making it easy to reach you is part of good website design: show your phone number, a WhatsApp button, a short form, the address of your practice with a map and your hours. Alongside these, a short FAQ can help you save time: it answers the questions patients ask you every week, such as how long the first consultation takes or what to bring.

Content: how to describe your nutrition services without promises

Describe what you do, not what the client will achieve. Clear content, written in plain language and without promises, helps clients understand your work and keeps your website within the advertising rules that apply to you. In a health profession, don't promise weight loss, timeframes or results, and avoid before-and-after photos. Use only the titles you actually hold, and discuss each person's goals during the session itself.

Here are some services you might describe, each with what it includes:

  • nutrition sessions, in person or by video call;
  • nutritional assessment and personalised eating plans;
  • nutritional advice and follow-up sessions;
  • nutrition education and healthy eating habits, including group sessions;
  • talks and group workshops for schools or companies;
  • sports nutrition;
  • nutrition for children and families.

Pricing: full prices, no offers

If you publish your pricing, show the full price of each session or plan and avoid offers, discounts and promotional prices. If you would rather not publish prices, it's best to explain how to ask for them. A clear price helps patients decide, and in a regulated profession it avoids anything that looks like a sales push: your website is there to inform, not to sell.

What happens at the first session

Explain what happens at the first session: what you assess, what the client should bring, such as previous medical reports or blood tests, and how follow-up appointments are organised. With a complete description, the client knows what to expect and you start with the right information.

A blog with reliable nutrition content

A blog or resources section with posts, guides and recipes shows your expertise and helps new clients find you through search engines. Share general, well-sourced nutrition information and remind readers that an article doesn't replace individual advice. Creating content takes time, so start with a simple content strategy: answer the questions your clients ask you again and again, one post at a time. Regular blog posts also give you something to share in your social media posts and email marketing. If writing isn't your thing, see how to write website content when you're not a writer.

Website design for nutritionists: clear, welcoming and easy to use

Good design means a clear, readable experience that is easy to use on a mobile phone: anyone should understand right away what you offer and how to reach you. Clear content matters more than visual effects.

Colours, fonts and layouts

Your brand is more than a logo: it is the colours, words and photos that show your personality and what working with you is like. Many website templates are built for wellness businesses in general rather than for nutrition practices; aim for the right tone, warm but serious, with your qualification as visible as your photos. A few design elements make a site feel welcoming and professional:

  • Two or three colours used consistently, easy to read against the background.
  • Readable fonts at a comfortable size, especially on mobile.
  • Clean layouts with white space, short paragraphs and clear headings.
  • Photos of you and your practice rather than generic stock images: they help people recognise you.
  • Buttons that look like buttons, with wording such as “Book a session”.

Simple navigation

Keep the menu short, for example Home, About, Services, Online sessions and Contact, and add a button to get in touch that stays visible on every page. Simple navigation lets visitors find what they need without having to think about it.

Built for mobile phones

Many of your clients will look you up on their mobile. Check that your phone number can be tapped to call, that your site loads quickly and that the form is easy to fill in on a small screen. Speed also counts for Google, as our article on website speed and search rankings explains.

Online nutrition sessions: how to explain them on your site

Video sessions let you see clients from other cities, so explain on your site how they work, which platform you use and how follow-up works between sessions. Nutritionists who combine appointments in person with online ones should present both options clearly. Cover each step:

  1. How to book: the button, link or phone number to use.
  2. What the client needs: a good connection and a quiet place.
  3. How the video call works, how long it lasts and how to prepare.
  4. How and when to pay.

If you offer follow-up plans, explain how patients can ask questions between sessions, how quickly you reply and when a new session makes more sense.

Clients beyond your city, and in other languages

If you're working remotely, say so: a nutritionist in Valencia can see people from other cities in Spain, but only if they know the option exists and how it works. If you work in English as well as Spanish, make it clear, so that English speakers looking for a nutritionist in their language can find you easily.

Data protection and legal texts on nutrition websites

Anyone who writes to a nutritionist may share medical information, which the GDPR treats as a special category of data, with stricter rules.

A contact form that asks only what is needed

A web form lets people write to you without calling. It's best to keep it short: a name, a phone number or email address and a short message are enough. Don't add a field asking why they want to see you or for medical information: it is sensitive, and you don't need it to arrange a first session.

What the legal texts cover

Legal texts are needed on any business website in Spain, under the LSSI and the GDPR:

  • Legal notice (aviso legal): identifies who runs the website, as the LSSI requires.
  • Data protection policy (política de privacidad): explains which data you collect, why, how long you keep it and how people can exercise their rights.
  • Cookie policy (política de cookies): explains which cookies the site uses.

Local SEO for dietitians: helping clients in your area find you

If you see clients in person, many will search nearby: “nutritionist in Valencia”, “dietitian in Elche” or “sports nutrition near me”. Your Google Business Profile and your website should both be ready for those searches.

Keywords with your town

Combine what you offer with your town or neighbourhood in your page titles and texts, such as “dietitian in Alicante”. Think about the words your patients actually type into the search box rather than technical terms. Common mistakes on dietitian websites

These mistakes appear on websites for dietitians and nutritionists alike, and they put off potential clients who were ready to get in touch:

  • Not showing your qualification or your colegiado number.
  • Not explaining how online sessions work or how to book.
  • Walls of text with no clear next step.
  • Hiding your phone number.
  • Promising weight loss or publishing offers.
  • Asking why someone wants to see you, or for medical information, in your form.
  • Leaving your Google Business Profile out of date, with old hours or photos.

Advice from other countries that doesn't fit Spain

Many guides for dietitians and nutritionists are written for the US market, and some of their advice doesn't apply here. Patient success stories are not allowed in Spanish health advertising, and your data protection rules are the GDPR and Spanish law, not American ones. Client portals and practice management software are presented as must-haves for nutrition practices, but a website doesn't need them: if you already use such tools, a link to them is enough.
