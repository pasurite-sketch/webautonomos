Local link building means getting other websites in your area to link to yours: the town hall's business guide, a trade association, a regional newspaper or the shop next door. It's word-of-mouth marketing in digital form: when the traders' association in Elche or the digital edition of Información links to your site, search engines see one more sign that your business is real and relevant where you operate.

This guide for freelancers and small businesses in Spain covers link building strategies and techniques based on relationships and useful content, from finding links to judging their quality, without buying a single link.

What is local link building?

Local link building is the part of off-page SEO that earns you backlinks from pages connected to your town or region. A backlink is simply a link from another site to one of your pages; what matters here is the connection between the linking page and the place where you work.

Backlinks, citations and mentions

  • Backlink: a clickable link from another site to yours, which can pass authority and provide traffic.
  • Citation: your name, address and phone number (NAP) on another site, with or without a link (see our guide to citations and NAP).
  • Unlinked mention: a page that names your brand without linking to it, often the easiest link to win.

What makes a link local?

In simple terms, any of three things: the site behind it belongs to an organisation or a company in your area, talks about your town or district, or has readers nearby. If the builders' merchant where a plumber in Elche buys materials lists them as a recommended installer, that link confirms the business and its trade in Elche.

Why do links from your area matter for SEO?

Because, today, links remain one of the ways a search engine judges a site's authority, and those from your area also confirm where you operate. The benefits go beyond SEO: such links bring referral traffic from people nearby who may need your services and see your brand recommended by a site they know, so link building in your area is community marketing too.

Prominence in Google's results

Google explains that its local results depend mainly on three factors:

  • Relevance: how well your business matches the search.
  • Distance: how far you are from the person searching, or from the place they mention.
  • Prominence: how well known your business is. Google says this also takes into account information it finds about a business across the web, such as links, articles and directories.

You can't move closer to every customer, but you can build prominence. Your website and your Google Business Profile go hand in hand: see how to appear in the Google Map Pack.

Directories, chambers of commerce and associations

These are the easiest links to get and a natural first step in any link building campaign: getting listed mostly depends on signing up or taking part, and a listing can stay online for years. They won't make a whole SEO strategy, but they can help you build citations.

Directories and citations

  • general directories such as Páginas Amarillas and QDQ;
  • niche directories for your trade, your professional association (colegio profesional) or your guild;
  • the trade guide of some town halls and the shopping portal of your district, if there is one.

Directories vary in quality, so do some research before you add your listing: is the directory indexed, visited and moderated? Enter your name, address and phone number the same way everywhere, exactly as on your Google Business Profile, because different data confuses search engines and customers alike.

Member directories and events

Some town councils, traders' associations, chambers of commerce and professional associations publish a directory of their members: check the ones in your town. In Valencia, Alicante, Elche or Elda, start with the organisations you already belong to and make sure your member profile links to your site: a valuable link that many members forget.

Chambers and associations also offer other opportunities, such as talks and trade fairs: if you take part as a speaker or partner, request a link on the event page. Some councils run a campaign to promote neighbourhood shops and list those taking part, sometimes with a link.

How do you get coverage in the regional press?

Give a newspaper content its audience will care about. The digital editions of papers such as Levante-EMV, Información or Las Provincias, radio stations and bloggers in your area link to businesses when there's a story to tell, so press coverage is free marketing as well as a link opportunity.

News stories a newspaper may want

  • the opening, move or expansion of your business;
  • a community initiative, such as a free workshop for residents;
  • seasonal advice: an air-conditioning technician in Alicante explaining how to prepare the unit for summer;
  • your expert opinion on new works, rules or anything going on in your town.

How to pitch a journalist

Send a short email that sums up the story in two or three sentences, says why it matters to readers in your area and gives the address of the page the article could link to. Provide a few of your best photos and a phone number so they can contact you. If they publish your content without a link, politely ask them to add one. Each article can also help build your personal brand, so journalists think of you next time they need an expert.

Create content that attracts links

The links that last are often the ones nobody had to request: someone found useful content on your site and decided to share it. That's the idea behind content marketing, and it suits a small business too. Creating content takes time, but a good page can keep attracting natural links for years.

A guide to your neighbourhood or town

Practical content about a place you know well can help far more people than your customers: walking routes put together by a physiotherapist in Valencia, or a neighbourhood guide for newcomers by an estate agent in Alicante. Associations, neighbourhood groups and bloggers like to share that kind of content.

Seasonal tips and resources for newcomers

Seasonal content is a great way to earn backlinks every year: think of a gardener in Elche explaining what to plant each season. If many of your customers moved to Spain from abroad, create a page of resources in plain English (registering on the padrón, finding a doctor, rubbish collection days): expat forums and community groups may link to that kind of content. If writing isn't your thing, see how to write website content when you're not a writer.

Guest posting on blogs in your region

A guest post is content you contribute to someone else's blog, usually with a short author bio that links to your site. It's a classic among link building techniques and an opportunity to reach potential customers, as long as it stays occasional and suits their audience.

Where to look for guest posting opportunities

  • niche blogs related to weddings, leisure or what's on in your town;
  • community websites for English speakers on the Costa Blanca;
  • podcasts and YouTube channels from your area, which may link to their guests.

How to write a guest post that gets accepted

Read a few of their articles first to see what content their audience likes, then propose one useful topic, not an advert. A hairdresser in Elda, for instance, could write about hairstyles for the Moros y Cristianos festivities. Keep a single link in the author bio, pointing to your home page or the relevant service page. And don't turn it into a routine: Google's spam policies list large-scale guest posting campaigns with keyword-rich anchor text among the examples of link spam.

Sponsor a club, a festival or an event

Sponsorship is a great way to support your community, and many clubs and festivals thank their sponsors on their website with a logo, a news item or a link.

Who to approach

  • grassroots football or basketball clubs and fun runs;
  • falla commissions, hogueras, comparsas and the residents' association;
  • markets, fairs and charity events.

Many of them only thank their sponsors on social networks such as Facebook and Instagram, which gives you visibility but little or no SEO value, so request a proper link on their website as well.

Paid sponsorship and the sponsored attribute

If you pay to appear, Google asks for the sponsored or nofollow attribute on the link, which it may then not count for ranking. The benefit is elsewhere: traffic, and your name in front of people nearby, which is useful marketing in itself.

Neighbouring trades, suppliers and partners

Your professional network is another source of backlinks: companies whose services complement yours and those you buy from. If you build relationships with them, a link often follows with little effort.

Complementary trades

  • an electrician, a plumber and a renovation company in Valencia;
  • a physiotherapist, a gym and a personal trainer in the same neighbourhood;
  • a hairdresser, a beauty salon and a wedding photographer.

Complementary trades often share customers, so they're a great fit: create joint content such as a guide, a partners page or a shared marketing campaign that everyone's customers benefit from. Keep it short, real and useful; a page that links to dozens of companies looks like an exchange, not a recommendation.

Suppliers and business customers

Many manufacturers and distributors have an installer finder or a stockists page: send an email to find out how to appear on it. You can also offer a short testimonial to a supplier you're happy with; some display them with the customer's name and a link.

Turn mentions and social media into links

Find unlinked mentions

Sometimes a site already talks about your business without linking to it. These brand mentions are a great place to start: the hard part is done, and every mention is an opportunity. To find them:

  • search for your brand name in quotes and exclude your own site with -site:yourdomain.es;
  • try creating a Google Alert for your name to get an email when new pages mention it;
  • look at the websites of events you've taken part in and festival programmes published online.

Then contact the author, thank them for the mention and ask for a link.

If they don't reply, remind them once some time later and leave it there: in that case the mention still counts as a citation.

Social media as a relay

Links on Facebook, Instagram or LinkedIn usually carry a nofollow attribute and pass almost no SEO authority, but social media still belongs in your marketing strategy: sharing your content puts it in front of journalists, bloggers and associations who may link to it from their own sites, and it can help build trust. LinkedIn can help you reach other companies. To choose your channels, see social media for the self-employed.

How to judge the quality of a link

In link building, a valuable link comes from a real site with visitors, related to your area or your niche, placed within an article and with natural wording. A link that ticks none of those boxes isn't worth your time.

Relevance, authority and real traffic

Search for site:theirdomain.es to see whether Google indexes it, then check that it's active, that real people visit it and that it's close to your trade. SEO tools calculate their own authority scores; Google doesn't use them, but they help you compare sites. A .es or .com ending matters less than relevance.

Anchor text, placement and nofollow

A link inside content that talks about you gives more context than one lost in a footer. The anchor, the clickable words, should vary between your brand name, your URL and, now and then, your service and your town. A natural mix matters, because the same keyword every time looks artificial (our guide to choosing keywords for your area helps). Google treats the nofollow, sponsored and ugc attributes as hints, and a nofollow link from a newspaper still brings visitors.

Link building mistakes to avoid

Google's spam policies forbid buying or selling links for ranking purposes, and some strategies sold as shortcuts do exactly that. At best, the algorithm ignores those links; with a manual action, your site can lose visibility in search results.

Practices to stay away from

  • buying links, or sponsored content without the sponsored attribute: be wary of any SEO agency offering hundreds of cheap links;
  • creating links with automated programs or services;
  • link farms and private blog networks: sites with no readers and thin content, created only to hand out links;
  • mass link exchanges (“you link to me and I'll link to you”);
  • low-quality directories and forum comments with optimised links;
  • the same exact-match anchor over and over, or a sudden wave of links from unrelated sites.

What happens after a manual action

A notice appears in the Manual actions report of Search Console; you then remove or disavow the problem links and request a review. In any case, if you've never bought links, you shouldn't need the disavow tool.

Track your link building and plan the next steps

The Links report in Search Console

Google Search Console is a free tool that shows your top linked pages, the sites that link to you and the anchors they use. Export the data each month to spot each new or lost link compared with the previous month; a new link only appears once the page that contains it has been crawled. Referral visits show up in Google Analytics: see how to know if your website works.

Your link building plan, step by step

Treat link building as part of your marketing, mix several strategies and keep things simple: a regular effort of half an hour a week is the easiest way to keep going.

  1. Get your site in order: name, phone and address identical to your Google Business Profile, and pages that explain your services and areas (Schema LocalBusiness can help a search engine understand them).
  2. Map your network and research your competitors: build a list of suppliers, customers, associations, clubs and newspapers, and see where the competitors who rank above you appear.
  3. Focus first on the easy links: quality directories, your association, the chamber and your suppliers.
  4. Create one piece of content worth linking to, then pitch a guest post or a story by email to blogs, newspapers and complementary trades.
  5. Measure your success every month and repeat what works.

Frequently asked questions

What is a local backlink, and why does it matter for SEO?
A local backlink is a link to your website from a site connected to your area, such as your town council, a regional newspaper or a nearby supplier. Search engines treat it as a vote of confidence, and Google says it considers information found across the web, such as links, articles and directories, when it judges how prominent a business is.
How many backlinks do I need to rank in my town?
There is no magic number: it depends on your trade, your area and the links your competitors have. Compare yourself with the businesses that rank above you instead of chasing a figure. A few relevant links from well-known sites in your area often count for more than many links from sites nobody has heard of.
Is buying backlinks risky for my business?
Yes. Google's spam policies forbid buying or selling links for ranking purposes, and they treat excessive link exchanges as link spam too. Such links may simply be ignored, or lead to a manual action that reduces your visibility until the problem is fixed.
How long does a backlink take to take effect?
There is no fixed delay. Google first has to crawl the page that links to you, and how soon that happens depends on how often it visits that page. The effect then builds up gradually, which is why link building is a medium to long-term strategy.
Can I earn backlinks without having a website?
