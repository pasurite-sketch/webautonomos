The e-invoicing mandate in France: deadlines, platforms and what changes in Spain

The first stage of the e-invoicing mandate in France took effect on 1 September 2026. Since then, every business established in France and subject to VAT must be able to receive electronic invoices, whatever its size, and large and mid-sized companies must also issue them and report their other transactions. SMEs and micro-businesses, sole traders included, start issuing and reporting on 1 September 2027. This guide covers who is concerned, how the system works and how to get ready, with a section for those who invoice between Spain and France and one on Spain's own rules.

What is the e-invoicing mandate in France?

The e-invoicing mandate is a French reform with two parts: B2B invoices, between businesses established in France, must be electronic, in a structured format, and go through platforms registered with the tax administration; for other transactions, the data must be reported to the tax authorities.

The French government's stated aims are to fight fraud, follow business activity in real time, speed up payments and, eventually, prefill VAT returns.

E-invoicing and e-reporting: two obligations

  • E-invoicing applies to sales between businesses established in France: each invoice is an e-invoice and travels through a platform.
  • E-reporting covers what e-invoicing does not, such as B2C sales and international transactions: businesses pass on transaction data, and payment data for services, to the tax authorities.

Both go through the same platform and start on the same date for each company size.

Who is concerned by the e-invoicing mandate?

Every business established in France and subject to VAT is concerned, whatever its size or legal form: companies, sole traders and micro-entrepreneurs alike. All of them must be able to receive e-invoices; issuing them becomes mandatory on a date that depends on company size.

The VAT franchise does not exempt you from e-invoicing

A frequent misunderstanding is that it does. A micro-entrepreneur under the franchise en base is still a taxable person, even without charging VAT, and it is being a taxable person, not charging the tax, that triggers the e-invoicing requirements. The "TVA non applicable" mention stays on your invoices; what changes is how they are transmitted.

Exempt operations: no e-invoices to send, but some to receive

Operations exempt from VAT, such as certain medical, paramedical or teaching services, are outside the obligation to issue e-invoices and the related e-reporting. A doctor or a physiotherapist remains a taxable person, though, and must still be equipped to receive their suppliers' electronic invoices. If your activity mixes exempt and taxable operations, ask your accountant how the rules apply to each part.

Businesses established outside France

A business with no establishment in France is outside this e-invoicing system, for sending and receiving alike. It may still have e-reporting obligations if it is registered for VAT there. If you invoice French clients from Spain, see the cross-border section below.

Key French e-invoicing deadlines in 2026 and 2027

The implementation is staged by company size, but receiving comes first for everyone:

ObligationLarge and mid-sized companiesSMEs and micro-businesses
Receive e-invoices1 September 20261 September 2026
Issue e-invoices1 September 20261 September 2027
E-reporting1 September 20261 September 2027

These dates were set by French finance law after an earlier implementation calendar was postponed.

For a small business, the result is a transition period: it receives electronic invoices from its larger suppliers but can keep sending its own invoices as before until its turn comes.

Which category is your company in?

The categories are the official French company size categories: large companies, mid-sized ones (entreprises de taille intermédiaire, ETI), SMEs and micro-businesses, based on headcount and financial figures. Sole traders, micro-entrepreneurs and small companies are in the second group; if you are close to a threshold, ask your accountant.

Who does what in the e-invoicing system?

Every business in scope needs a platform to exchange its electronic invoices, because the public portal (PPF) does not carry invoices between businesses: it runs the directory and collects data for the tax authorities.

Approved platforms (formerly PDP)

These platforms, plateformes agréées in French, were first called PDP (plateformes de dématérialisation partenaires). They are private service providers registered by the French tax administration, the Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), or Directorate General of Public Finances. Your platform:

  • issues your e-invoices and delivers them to the businesses you invoice;
  • receives those your suppliers send you;
  • runs compliance checks on each invoice: complete data, accepted format;
  • passes invoice data, transaction data and statuses to the PPF.

The DGFiP publishes the official list on impots.gouv.fr. Your invoicing or accounting software may be one of these platforms, or connect to one in the background: ask your vendor.

The PPF: directory and data for the DGFiP

The public invoicing portal (portail public de facturation, PPF) was first meant to offer a free solution for exchanging invoices, but the French government changed course.

Today it runs the central directory, which records each business's chosen platform and receiving address, and gathers the data passed on by the platforms before it reaches the DGFiP. You will rarely deal with it directly: your provider registers you there.

How an e-invoice travels

  1. You create the invoice in your software, which passes it to your platform.
  2. The platform checks it, finds your client in the directory and forwards it to the client's provider.
  3. Your client receives it, and statuses come back as the invoice is accepted, refused or paid.
  4. Along the way, the required data goes to the PPF, then to the tax authorities.

Accepted formats: what counts as an e-invoice

A French e-invoice must use one of three structured formats that software can read without retyping. A simple PDF sent by email is not an e-invoice under the mandate, even when your software generated it.

  • Factur-X: a hybrid file, a document people can read with the invoice data embedded in it.
  • UBL (Universal Business Language): an XML standard also used for e-invoicing elsewhere in Europe.
  • CII (Cross Industry Invoice): an XML standard from UN/CEFACT.

You don't need to read XML: compliant software handles the format for you. What matters is that yours meets the requirements: it should produce and accept at least one of these formats, with complete invoice information.

Additional mandatory mentions

The reform also adds four mentions to French invoices:

  • the customer's SIREN number, the French company identification number;
  • the delivery address of goods, when it differs from the customer's address;
  • whether the invoice covers goods, services or both;
  • the option for paying VAT on debits, if you have chosen it.

Update your template and client records now: the SIREN is also how a business is found in the directory.

What does e-reporting cover?

E-reporting is the transmission of data to the tax authorities for transactions that e-invoicing does not cover. It follows the same calendar as the obligation to issue e-invoices, by company size.

  • B2C transactions: sales to private customers, which need no e-invoice but whose data must be reported.
  • International transactions: sales to businesses outside France and purchases from foreign suppliers.
  • Payment data: for services, the amounts actually collected, since the tax on them generally becomes due when you are paid.

Your platform passes this data on with the rest, and the frequency depends on your tax regime. A micro-entrepreneur who only sells to private customers therefore still has e-reporting to do from 2027, even without business clients.

Statuses: tracking the life of each e-invoice

Every e-invoice carries a status that shows where it stands, from submission to settlement. Four statuses are mandatory and also reach the tax authorities:

  • Submitted (déposée): the invoice has reached the seller's platform.
  • Rejected (rejetée): it was turned down for a technical reason, such as a wrong format or an unknown recipient.
  • Refused (refusée): the buyer disagrees with it, for example over the amount or an order it does not recognise.
  • Paid (encaissée): the seller confirms it has been paid, which provides the data on payments.

Other statuses, such as in dispute, are optional but useful: they show whether an invoice is moving or stuck. A rejected or refused invoice has to be corrected and sent again, and the difference tells you who needs to act: you and your software, or you and your client.

B2G: Chorus Pro is already mandatory

If you invoice French public bodies, such as ministries, local authorities or hospitals, nothing changes: B2G e-invoicing has been mandatory for several years for every supplier, micro-businesses included, through the government's Chorus Pro portal. The new mandate does not replace it: your B2G invoices keep going through Chorus Pro.

Invoicing between Spain and France

Under the French mandate, what matters is where each party is established: e-invoicing only applies when both are established in France.

You are established in Spain and invoice French clients

If your business is established in Spain, with no establishment in France, the French obligation does not apply to you. A translator in Alicante who works for agencies in Lyon, for example, keeps invoicing under Spanish rules, including Verifactu for the software used, and those agencies will report the purchases through e-reporting.

If you have a French VAT number, or premises or staff in France, check your situation with an adviser.

You are established in France and invoice Spanish clients

Your invoices to clients established in Spain stay outside e-invoicing, but their data must be reported once your own deadline arrives. Wherever your customers are based, you still need a platform to receive your French suppliers' e-invoices. What changes in your daily work

Day to day, the key change is that supplier bills stop arriving by email: they reach you through your platform, with their data already readable by your systems.

Receiving: one channel for e-invoices

Incoming e-invoices land in your e-invoicing solution or in the accounting tool connected to it, where someone checks, approves or refuses each one.

Sending: from your software to your client

Once your deadline arrives, each invoice to a French business client goes through your platform and comes back with statuses. Sales to private customers and to foreign clients are reported, not e-invoiced.

Less retyping, if you use the data

The real benefit is not compliance, it is what compliance makes possible. An e-invoice no longer has to be keyed in again: the data arrives readable by your system, so invoice processing, filing and payment tracking can stop being manual. Many firms will install a tool to comply, then carry on copying everything by hand, and miss the part that mattered.

How to prepare for the e-invoicing mandate

To be ready, start with your software, then choose your e-invoicing solution and organise who handles invoices:

  1. Confirm your dates and your company category with your accountant.
  2. Ask your software vendor whether your tool meets the French requirements (accepted formats, connection to a platform): many vendors have rolled out updates without telling their customers.
  3. Choose your provider and sign up, so that suppliers can find you in the directory.
  4. Update your data: client SIREN numbers, invoice templates with the new mentions, numbering.
  5. Organise reception and validation: decide who receives incoming bills, who approves them and how errors get fixed.
  6. Test before your deadline with a few invoices, so that rejections show up before they matter.

The organisation of reception and validation is often what is missing, and no tool solves it on its own.

Choosing your e-invoicing provider: what to check

  • it appears on the list published by the DGFiP;
  • it connects to your invoicing software and to your accountant's systems;
  • it covers every flow: sending, receiving, transaction data and statuses;
  • it stores your invoices and lets you export your data;
  • its price fits your invoice volume, and its support is easy to reach.

Penalties and practical risks

French law provides for fines per invoice not issued electronically and per missing e-reporting transmission; ask your accountant for the current amounts.

The more immediate risk is practical: e-invoices from your suppliers that never reach you, bills paid late and a rushed fix afterwards.

Spain: Verifactu and Crea y Crece, two separate rules

Unlike the French mandate, the Spanish obligations come from two separate rules, often confused, with different scopes and calendars.

Verifactu: requirements for your invoicing software

Verifactu governs the invoicing software itself and covers everything you invoice with it, including sales to private customers:

  • each invoice produces a chained, unalterable billing record and carries a QR code;
  • records are either sent to the Spanish Tax Agency (AEAT) or kept with stricter security requirements;
  • the manufacturer must sign a declaración responsable, its statement of compliance.

It does not apply to:

  • businesses that already use the SII;
  • those whose fiscal address is in the Basque Country or Navarre, which have their own rules;
  • those who invoice by hand.

Crea y Crece: e-invoices between businesses

The Crea y Crece law (Ley de creación y crecimiento de empresas) sets three requirements for invoices between businesses and professionals:

  • an electronic, structured format such as Facturae;
  • sending through digital channels;
  • the status of each invoice (accepted, rejected or paid), reported to fight late payment.

B2C invoices are not covered, and those to Spanish public administrations already go through FACe.

Spanish dates have moved more than once

Both Spanish rules have been postponed, and the rules for their implementation have kept changing. Entry into force is staged: the Verifactu calendar has separated companies from other taxpayers, and Crea y Crece starts with the largest companies, followed a year later by everyone else, freelancers included.

Check the latest official publication from the AEAT or ask your gestor before planning anything: dates circulating online are often out of date. Our guide to mandatory electronic invoicing for freelancers in Spain covers the Spanish side in more detail.

France and Spain at a glance

  • France: a single reform, with fixed dates in 2026 and 2027.
  • Spain: Verifactu for software and Crea y Crece for B2B e-invoices, with staged dates to check with the AEAT.
  • Both: only a structured format counts as an e-invoice.

Frequently asked questions

I am a sole trader in France. Does the e-invoicing mandate apply to me?
Yes, if your business is established in France and subject to VAT, even under the franchise en base: you are a taxable person even if you don't charge the tax. Receiving e-invoices has been mandatory since 1 September 2026; sending them and e-reporting follow on 1 September 2027. Exempt services, such as certain medical work, need no e-invoice, but you must still receive your suppliers' e-invoices.
Does a PDF count as an electronic invoice?
No, in neither country. France requires a structured format readable by a machine (Factur-X, UBL or CII), and Spain's Crea y Crece requires formats such as Facturae. A document meant only for people to read is not enough, even when a program generated it.
What is the difference between e-invoicing and e-reporting?
E-invoicing covers domestic B2B invoices, between businesses established in France, which must be sent and received electronically through a platform. E-reporting covers the rest, such as sales to private customers and international transactions: the business passes the transaction data to the tax authorities, on the same calendar.
Do I need an e-invoicing platform just to receive invoices?
Yes. In France, the public portal does not exchange invoices between businesses, so every business established in France and subject to VAT needs a platform to receive electronic invoices, even before it has to send them. The provider also registers the business in the national directory, so that suppliers know where to send its e-invoices.
What happens if I do nothing before the deadline?
In France, your suppliers' e-invoices need a platform to reach you, so without one they stop arriving through the usual channels and your bills get paid late. There are also fines per invoice not issued electronically and per missing e-reporting transmission; ask your accountant for the current amounts.
Will I have to change my accounting software for e-invoicing?
Not necessarily. Many vendors have already updated their products, or connected them to an e-invoicing service, without their customers noticing. Ask your current provider first: it can be cheaper and simpler than starting again with another tool.
How much does an approved platform cost?
Prices vary with invoice volume and the features chosen, and some business software now includes the function at no extra cost. Compare what your current vendor offers before signing up elsewhere.
Does the French mandate apply if I invoice French clients from Spain?
Not as such: French e-invoicing applies to businesses established in France. If yours is established in Spain, your invoices follow Spanish rules, such as Verifactu, and your French business clients will declare those purchases themselves. If you have a French VAT number, check your situation with an adviser.
