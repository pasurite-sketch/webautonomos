Yes: website speed affects SEO. Google takes loading speed and other page experience signals into account when it ranks pages, and a slow site also loses visitors before they see what you offer. Speed won't make up for weak content, but between two similar pages, the quicker one has the edge. In this article, we explain without the jargon what is measured, how to test your own website for free and how to make it load quicker, step by step.

How does page speed influence SEO?

The short answer: page speed affects SEO in two ways. Directly, because Google's core ranking systems take loading speed into account; and indirectly, because slow pages lose visitors, which means fewer calls, messages and enquiries for you.

A direct ranking factor

Google has used speed as a signal for years, first for desktop searches and later for mobile searches too. Today it is part of what Google calls page experience: how it feels to use a website, not just what it says. It covers a few factors:

  • Loading: how long it takes for the main content to load.
  • Interactivity: how soon a button reacts when a user taps it.
  • Visual stability: whether the layout stays still while the page loads.
  • Security: whether your website is served over HTTPS.
  • Ease of use on a phone: text that is legible and no intrusive pop-ups covering the screen.

We explain below how the first three are measured.

An indirect effect through user experience

Speed also matters for SEO through your visitors. When a page takes several seconds to load, many users give up and click on another result. They never see the information about your services or your phone number. A quicker website keeps them around until they find what they came for.

What a slow website costs a small company

If you're a plumber in Elche or a hairdresser in Alicante, your website is your digital shop window, and the impact is concrete: if it doesn't load straight away, the visitor calls the next name on the list. The same goes for an online store, where every slow product page makes buying harder. Slowness also undermines the rest of your online marketing and SEO: whether visitors arrive from a search engine, social media or a digital marketing campaign, they all end up in the same place, and every extra second of waiting gives them a reason to leave. The traffic you worked or paid for is lost before it turns into an enquiry, so the delay costs you twice, in rankings and in customers.

How important is speed compared with content?

Page speed is one of many ranking factors, and relevance comes first. Google has explained that the intent behind a search remains a very strong signal, so a slow page with great, relevant content can still rank higher than a fast one that misses the point. Where speed makes a difference is between pages of similar quality, such as your website and the websites of your competitors in Valencia or Alicante. What are Core Web Vitals?

They are three metrics that describe the real user experience of a page: how fast its main content loads (LCP), how quickly it responds to a tap or click (INP) and how stable its layout is while it loads (CLS).

They are based on the visits of real Chrome users, collected in the Chrome User Experience Report. For each metric, Google's documentation sets a threshold for a good experience, and PageSpeed Insights and Search Console rate each page as good, needs improvement or poor. Understanding what each one measures will help you decide where to start.

Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): loading

LCP measures how long the largest element in the visible part of the screen, often the main image or headline, takes to load. Heavy images, a slow server and render-blocking resources, such as stylesheets (CSS) and scripts that the browser has to download before it can display anything, all increase it.

Interaction to Next Paint (INP): interactivity

INP measures how quickly the screen responds when a user taps a button, opens a menu or types in a form. If nothing happens for a moment after someone taps 'Call', the INP is poor, and too much JavaScript is often behind it. INP replaced an older metric, First Input Delay (FID), which only looked at the first interaction.

Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): visual stability

CLS measures how much the layout shifts without warning: a user is about to tap a link and everything jumps down because a photo or a banner has just loaded. You prevent it by reserving space for each element from the start, for example by giving every image its width and height.

Field data and lab data

There are two ways to collect speed data. Field data comes from real users: Google's reports provide it when your site gets enough visits, and it is what counts for your rankings. Lab data comes from a simulated test that runs on demand: it doesn't capture the conditions of your real visitors, but it helps you find problems and test a fix straight away. A small site with few visits may only have lab data, which is normal.

Why does speed matter even more on mobile?

Many of your customers will look you up online from their phone, often on data rather than wifi, with a device that has less processing power than a computer, so a heavy page takes longer to load. On top of that, Google mainly uses the mobile version of your content for indexing and ranking. So page speed on a phone is a factor in your rankings, and your visitors decide within seconds whether to stay.

What visitors on a phone need

A phone-friendly website meets a few basics:

  • Images resized for the screen, which load faster than the original photo from the camera.
  • Few scripts, so buttons respond to the first tap.
  • Large buttons and text you can read without zooming.
  • No pop-up blocking the screen as soon as you arrive.

How to check your website speed for free

Start with PageSpeed Insights, Google's free page speed test: enter the address of a page and you get a performance score, the LCP, INP and CLS results and suggestions on what to improve. For your whole website, add Search Console as well.

PageSpeed Insights: Google's free test

Paste your URL: the tool analyses it in a few seconds and gives you this information:

  • Real-user data at the top, if your site gets enough visits: this is what counts for SEO.
  • The lab score below it, a simulation that helps you find problems. A high lab score doesn't always reflect what real visitors see.
  • The diagnostics, which show what slows loading down and what to improve first: photos, code, fonts or the hosting.
  • The mobile tab, which simulates a modest phone on a slower connection, so it's normal for the desktop tab to look healthier. What's important is green on that tab for your key pages.

Search Console: a view of your whole website

Search Console is free too. A dedicated report groups the URLs of your site into good, needs improvement and poor, separately for phones and computers. Unlike a one-off test, it lets you follow every URL over time, and it can email you when a new issue appears. If your website has few visitors, the report may be empty.

Lighthouse and other testing tools

For a more detailed analysis, online tools such as GTmetrix or WebPageTest show the size and loading order of each file, including fonts and scripts. Lighthouse, built into the Chrome web browser, runs a similar lab test on any URL and can provide a performance report, which will help you find problems before you publish a change. Pick the tool that suits you best and stick with it, because each one calculates its scores in its own way.

The simplest test: your own phone

Finally, do a test that no tool replaces: open your website on your phone, away from wifi, the way a customer would, and watch your home page load. Is it fast enough? If you have to wait, so do your customers.

What page speed score should you aim for?

There is no single score to reach. What counts for SEO is that real visits pass Google's thresholds for LCP, INP and CLS; the lab score is a guide to get there.

In PageSpeed Insights, the performance score is shown in green, orange or red. Aim for green, but a lower score isn't a penalty in itself for your rankings: on some pages, factors such as a map, a video or a chat window can pull the score down while users notice no delay. It's best to compare each page with its own earlier scores rather than with other websites, and to follow the trend over time. What slows a website down?

A handful of factors affect page speed, and many are easy to improve without rebuilding the whole site:

What you notice Likely cause Metric
The main image appears late Heavy or unoptimised images LCP
The screen stays blank for a while A slow or overloaded server LCP
Buttons take a moment to react Too many scripts, plugins or widgets INP
Text jumps while the page loads Elements with no reserved space CLS
Fine on a computer, slow on a phone A page that is too heavy overall LCP and INP

If your site runs on WordPress, the problem often builds up over time: each plugin adds its own code, and each page takes longer to load than it needs to. If you're still choosing a platform, see our comparison Wix or WordPress.

What to fix first, step by step

You don't need to rebuild your website to improve page speed, and improving it works best one step at a time: go through these steps in order and check each change with the same tool.

Test before you change anything

Test the page speed of your important web pages (home page, services and contact) and write down how they score on a phone: the slowest ones are the first to improve. That starting point will tell you what works, so repeat the test after each change. If a web developer looks after your site, share the report with them.

Improve your images

They are often the first thing to optimise to reduce load time, and making them lighter is rarely complicated:

  • Resize each image to the size at which it is displayed.
  • Compress it to reduce its file size, and use a modern format such as WebP, which offers better compression than a JPG of similar quality.
  • Use lazy loading for images further down the page: they only load when the visitor reaches them.
  • Give every image its width and height, so the browser reserves its space.

Remove what you don't need

Every plugin, script and font can increase the number of files a browser has to download, so reducing them lightens each page:

  • Plugins: uninstall the ones you don't use, after checking that your site still works without them.
  • Third-party scripts (chats, maps, videos, social buttons, comment systems): each one adds weight, so keep only the important ones.
  • JavaScript and CSS: defer the scripts that aren't needed straight away, and minify your CSS and HTML files.
  • Fonts: best practice is to use few font families, served from your own domain.

Use caching and compression

Caching keeps ready-made copies of your files so they don't have to be rebuilt or downloaded again. Server caching stores the generated HTML of each page, and browser caching lets returning users reuse the images, CSS and scripts they have already downloaded, so they load your site sooner on their next visit. Compression (Gzip or Brotli) reduces the size of what travels over the network. Together, they improve load times for every visitor; on WordPress, a single caching plugin is enough.

Choose fast hosting and consider a CDN

If the diagnostics show a high server response time, improving your hosting comes first: a slow server holds back every page on your site. On cheap shared web hosting, your website runs on the same servers as many other websites and can suffer when they are busy, so look beyond the price when choosing a provider. A content delivery network (CDN) can also help: it keeps copies of your files on servers in many locations, so visitors load them from one close to where they are.

Common mistakes when optimising site speed

  • Obsessing over the performance score: what's important is the user experience of real users.
  • Measuring only on a computer and forgetting to test your site on a phone.
  • Comparing scores from one testing tool to another, since each calculates them differently.
  • Installing multiple optimisation plugins at once: they can conflict and break the design or the way the site works.
  • Lazy loading the main image at the top of the screen, which delays the LCP.
  • Expecting speed alone to do the job, when relevance comes first.

Speed and the rest of your technical SEO

Behind the scenes, SEO also involves the work that can help search engines find, crawl and understand your website, and issues there can hold back your rankings and organic traffic. Speed also has an impact on crawling: Google's search engine adjusts how much it crawls to how quickly your server responds, although this is mainly an issue for large sites. It's also worth looking at a few other basics, including:

  • An SSL certificate, so your site loads over HTTPS.
  • A responsive web design that works on a phone as well as on a computer.
  • Indexing: making sure each important page is indexed, which you can verify in Search Console.
  • Structured data that describes your company, as explained in our guide to LocalBusiness schema.

Frequently asked questions

Does page speed affect SEO?
Yes. Google has taken it into account for years and now assesses it on real visits with LCP, INP and CLS. It doesn't replace useful content, but between similar pages, a faster one has a better chance of appearing higher, especially on mobile.
What are LCP, INP and CLS?
LCP, INP and CLS are the three metrics Google uses to assess a page: LCP shows when the main content appears, INP how quickly the page responds when a user taps or clicks, and CLS whether elements move while it loads. You can see them for free in PageSpeed Insights and Search Console.
How can I check my website speed for free?
Go to PageSpeed Insights, paste your URL and look at the mobile tab: you'll see a performance score, your LCP, INP and CLS results and suggestions. To follow your progress over time, also use Google Search Console. Both are free.
Why is my website slow?
Frequent causes are heavy images, templates full of features you don't use, too many plugins or third-party scripts, and slow hosting. To improve load times, start with your photos, turn on compression and remove what you don't need.
Will a faster website put me at the top of Google?
Not on its own: it is one factor among many. The search engine looks first at relevance. Between results of similar quality, being quicker can tip the balance, and it keeps visitors around once they arrive.
