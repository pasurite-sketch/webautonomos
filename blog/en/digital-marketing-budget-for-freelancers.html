It is the question every freelancer asks sooner or later: how much should I spend on digital marketing? There is no magic number, but there is a method. Setting a marketing budget for a small business starts from your goals: how many new clients you need, how much profit each one leaves you and what you can afford to pay to win them. A plumber in Elda who has just registered is not in the same position as a dentist in Valencia with 10 years of loyal patients. This guide for freelancers and small businesses in Spain shows you how to calculate your budget, split it between your website, SEO, content, social media, email and advertising, and measure the return.

What is a digital marketing budget?

A digital marketing budget is the money you set aside each month or year to build your digital presence and promote what you do online. Part of your wider marketing budget, which may also cover flyers or signs on your van, it breaks down into four parts:

  • Fixed expenses: your website, your domain and the digital tools you subscribe to.
  • Services: the help you pay for, such as profile management, local SEO or design.
  • Variable spend: advertising, including boosted social media posts, which you can switch on and off.
  • Your hours: the effort you put into publishing, replying to reviews and updating your site.

Why does a small business need a marketing budget?

A small business needs a marketing budget because it turns digital marketing from a string of impulse purchases into a strategy you can measure. Without one, spending follows whatever offer arrives that week, and it is hard to know what actually brought in sales.

  • You stop buying on impulse. Directory listings, a sponsored mention, an advert in a local magazine: a monthly figure linked to your goals lets you turn down offers that do not fit your marketing strategy.
  • You can compare channels. Once you know what you spend on each one and how many leads it brings, you can move money towards the ones that pay for themselves.
  • Planning becomes easier. You decide in advance when to add a service, when to test a campaign and how much to keep for your busy season.

How much should a small business spend on digital marketing?

There is no single right amount: what you need to invest in marketing depends on how many customers you want and what you can afford to pay to win each one. For a freelancer starting out, that can mean a few dozen euros a month plus some of your own effort; small budgets can work if they are focused. A company with staff and bigger growth plans is likely to need more.

Percentage of revenue: a rough guide, not a rule

Many guides suggest that businesses set their marketing budgets as a fixed percentage of revenue. These benchmarks vary widely by industry, country and stage, and may describe companies in a very different market from a one-person business in Alicante.

A new company that needs to make itself known often has to spend more at first than an established one with plenty of referrals, so use a percentage, if at all, only as a sense check.

Factors that determine your budget

  • Your business goals: more enquiries, a new service, a second location or simply a fuller diary.
  • What a customer is worth: the profit from one job and whether people come back or recommend you.
  • Your audience: individuals, other businesses or both, since they search and decide in different ways.
  • Your industry and the competition: how many competitors appear in a local search for your service.
  • Your stage: a company that has just opened needs more brand awareness than one already known locally.

How to calculate your marketing budget in five steps

Work backwards from what you want your marketing to achieve; a notepad or a simple spreadsheet is enough.

Step one: set clear business goals

Write down what your digital marketing should achieve over the next three months in specific terms: calls, form requests, bookings or jobs. “More customers” is not a goal; “4 extra jobs a month from people in Elche” is. If you have just opened, brand awareness in your neighbourhood may come first. Goals help you see what success looks like and when to change your marketing strategy.

Step two: calculate what a customer is worth

Use profit, not revenue: look at what an average job leaves you, and remember that a patient who returns for several sessions, or someone who recommends you, is worth more than a single visit. This figure will determine how much you can spend to win a customer.

Step three: decide what you can pay to win each customer

This is your maximum customer acquisition cost: what you are prepared to spend on marketing to gain one paying customer. It has to stay well below the profit that customer leaves. A little research helps: look at what competitors in your local market offer and where they advertise.

Step four: multiply the jobs you need by that amount

Jobs needed × what you can pay per job = your monthly marketing budget. For example, if you want 4 extra jobs a month and are happy to pay up to €50 for each, your budget is about €200 a month. If each job leaves around €300 in profit on average, the numbers work comfortably.

Step five: check the total against your cash flow and split it

Make sure the figure fits what you can afford today, then divide it between fixed marketing costs that keep running every month, such as your website, your profile and local SEO, and variable spending such as ads, which you can pause. If the total is too high for now, lower the goal and increase the budget in line with your growth.

Hidden marketing costs: your time and your tools

The price of a service is only part of what marketing takes from you: your hours, your subscriptions and a few one-off payments belong in the budget too.

Your hours

Every hour spent on marketing tasks, such as editing photos or answering reviews, is an hour you are not doing billable jobs, so estimate those hours each month and give them a value.

When a task needs technical experience, such as setting up ad campaigns, or keeps you from paid jobs, asking for help may be the cheaper option.

Software and subscriptions

Marketing tools such as an email platform, a design app or a scheduling app each look inexpensive, but recurring costs add up: check the pricing of each plan before you sign up and cancel what you no longer use. Some essentials are free: Google Analytics and Search Console help you see how people find your website.

How to measure the ROI of your digital marketing

Track how many leads each channel brings, how many of them buy and your acquisition cost per sale. If winning a customer takes less than the profit they leave, your marketing pays for itself. Our article on measuring your website results explains the basic metrics.

Key metrics to track

  • Source: where each enquiry came from; ask everyone who contacts you how they heard about you and note it in a spreadsheet.
  • Leads: calls, messages, form requests and bookings, counted for each channel.
  • Conversion: how many of those leads turn into sales.
  • Customer acquisition cost: what you spent on a channel divided by the number of customers it brought.
  • Revenue and profit: what those sales brought in, and what was left after expenses.

How to calculate ROI

ROI (return on investment) compares what your digital marketing earns with what you spend on it: (profit generated − marketing spend) / marketing spend × 100. Imagine a painter in Elche who spends €59 a month and wins 4 jobs that month, each leaving an average profit of €300: his ROI is 1,933%, so every euro spent brings back about €20 in profit. These figures are illustrative, but the calculation stays the same for yours.

When to adjust your marketing budget

Review your marketing budget every three months, based on the performance of each channel, and adjust it according to the data: invest more in the strategies that bring sales at a price you can afford, and cut or change what does not.

Signs it is time to spend more on marketing

  • A channel brings customers for much less than the profit they leave.
  • Your growth is held back by a lack of enquiries rather than a lack of time.
  • You are launching a new service, or competition in your local market is increasing.
  • Your busy season is coming: planning for seasonal trends, such as air conditioning in summer, keeps you visible when demand rises.

Signs you should cut back or change strategy

  • Your campaigns have run for several weeks without a single lead.
  • Enquiries arrive but rarely turn into sales: check your website, your pricing and how quickly you reply before spending more.

Judge each channel over the right period: ads show their impact quickly, while SEO and content often take several months to build up.

Marketing budget mistakes small businesses make

These are common ways to waste part of a marketing budget:

  • Paying for ads before your website and profile are ready to turn visitors into enquiries.
  • Copying the budget or strategies of a competitor or of larger companies without knowing their goals or their results.
  • Spreading small budgets across too many tactics and platforms at once.
  • Judging success by likes and followers instead of enquiries and sales.
  • Stopping SEO after a few weeks, before it has had a chance to deliver.

Is paying for Google Ads worth it as a freelancer?
It depends on your sector and on the profit each job leaves you. If a single job is worth a lot, paid ads can pay for themselves; if not, begin with your profile, your website and local SEO, which keep running without a fee per click. What percentage of revenue should a small business spend on marketing?
No percentage of revenue suits all businesses: published benchmarks vary by sector, size and stage, and a new business often needs to spend more at first. For a local freelancer, it is more useful to calculate how many new clients you need and what you can afford to pay to win each one.
Can I do digital marketing for free?
Partly. Creating your Google listing, asking happy customers for reviews, publishing on social media and replying quickly to messages require no money, only hours. When those hours start to keep you from paid jobs, delegating some tasks may be worth it.
How do I know whether my marketing spend is working?
Track three indicators: how many calls, messages or form requests reach you through your website and your profile, how many become sales, and what each new client costs you to win. If that amount is lower than the profit they leave, your marketing is working.
