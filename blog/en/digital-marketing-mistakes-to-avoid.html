Marketing mistakes rarely announce themselves: your pages seem fine, your profiles exist, yet the calls don't come. Whether you're a freelancer (autónomo) or running a small business in Spain, from a plumber in Elche to a dental clinic in Valencia, you'll probably recognise a few of the ten mistakes below in your online presence. Each comes with a practical way to avoid it.

Which marketing mistakes hold small businesses back?

Ten common mistakes hold back many small businesses. They fall into four groups:

  • Planning: no strategy, no measurable goals, no clear picture of your customers.
  • Foundations: pages that don't convert, local SEO left aside, an abandoned Maps listing.
  • Channels: too many networks, posts without a plan, no newsletter.
  • Follow-through: untracked ads, no measurement, giving up too early, an inconsistent brand.

Marketing without a strategy or clear goals

Posting on Instagram one week and handing out flyers the next isn't a strategy: it's guesswork. Without a plan, you can't tell which actions bring in work and which simply eat your time.

Set goals you can measure

Decide what success looks like: a number and a deadline, such as ten more enquiries a month from Alicante by the summer. A simple plan fits on one page: what you're selling, who to, where those people look for it and the time you can give it. A small business doesn't need the methods of big companies.

Get to know your customers

Not knowing whether your ideal customer is a family or a property management company can lead to vague messages that convince no one. How to fix it: ask your last few clients why they chose you. Their answers help you understand your target audience and give you the words to convince the next ones.

A website that doesn't convert

Some business owners still have no website and rely on a Facebook page, so potential customers who search for them are likely to find a competitor. Others have pages that look fine but send visitors away: no contact form or WhatsApp button, text that's hard to read on a mobile, a load time over 3 seconds.

How to fix it: open your site on your mobile and send yourself an enquiry. A good design puts your services, your town and your details where people can see them in seconds.

Neglecting local SEO

If your pages never mention your town, you're competing with the whole of Spain rather than the 5-10 rivals in your area, and search engines have little reason to show you to potential clients nearby.

How to fix it: name your service and the town you target in your page titles and texts ('emergency plumber in Elche'), keep the same name and address everywhere and ask local associations or suppliers for a link. This can help search engines understand where you work. Results can take several months, so begin early; our guide to local SEO for freelancers covers each step.

A half-finished Google Business Profile

Many businesses never claim the profile that may already exist for their company, or claim it and forget it: no photos, no posts, no replies. Reviews are important: many potential clients read them before hiring, and if a competitor has 45 reviews at 4.8 stars while you have 3, it's easy to guess who gets the call.

How to fix it: claim and verify your profile, choose the right category, add accurate details and good photos of real jobs, keep it updated and reply to every review.

Being on every social media platform

Opening accounts everywhere, from Instagram to TikTok and X, feels productive, but it's a common trap: keeping that many accounts alive is hard while you're working, especially without a team. A profile whose latest post is 8 months old runs the risk of making people wonder whether you're still trading.

How to fix it: choose the one or two social media platforms that suit you best, where your audience already is: Instagram for visual trades, Facebook for local communities, LinkedIn if you sell to other companies. Then post at a pace you can keep; this guide on which networks are worth it can help.

Creating content without a plan

Three posts in one week followed by two months of silence tells visitors nobody is minding the shop.

How to fix it: set a realistic pace, such as one post a week and one article a month, and keep your ideas in a simple calendar. Answer the questions you're asked every week, and turn each finished job into a photo and a few lines on your pages. Content that's quick to create, published regularly, makes a difference to your growth.

Overlooking email marketing

Email can feel old-fashioned, so many freelancers never collect a single address. Yet a mailing list is a channel you own, which means no feed decides who sees your message, and a short reminder at the right moment can help past clients think of you first.

How to fix it: when you finish a job, simply ask whether you can stay in touch, then send a short message with real value every month or two: seasonal tips, availability or a new service.

Paying for ads without tracking the results

Some freelancers spend 200-500 euros a month on digital ads while their pages have no enquiry form and their Maps listing is half empty. Visitors arrive, find no easy way to get in touch and leave.

How to fix it: get the basics working first and estimate what a new customer is worth before setting a budget. Then start small and track each lead: calls, forms and WhatsApp messages.

With paid search ads you pay for each click, which means you risk paying for visits that never become calls: if a campaign brings no enquiries, pause it and fix the page it sends people to.

Measuring nothing and giving up too early

If you've never opened Google Analytics or Search Console, you don't know how many people visit your pages or which one brings enquiries. The opposite mistake is common too: dropping an action after a few weeks, before it had time to show results.

How to fix it: check a few key numbers once a month:

  • visits and their sources;
  • calls, forms and WhatsApp messages, and the channel behind each;
  • the searches your business appears in;
  • new reviews.

Both tools are free and help you understand what works best, so you can improve or drop the rest. Give each action time: local SEO and content can take several months, while ads can be judged sooner. Our guide to measuring your website results explains how to set up both tools.

An inconsistent brand

A different logo on your van, your site and your Instagram profile, a company name that changes from one directory to the next, a tone that's formal here and chatty there: each inconsistency makes your brand harder to recognise and to trust.

How to fix it: define a simple brand kit (name, logo, two or three colours, images in the same style, a short description) and use the same design everywhere so you stay recognisable.

How should a small business prioritise its marketing?

Begin with the foundations, then add digital channels one at a time rather than doing everything at once:

  1. Write down one goal and the customers you want to reach.
  2. Make sure your website shows what you do, your area and how to reach you.
  3. Claim and complete your Google Business Profile.
  4. Pick one or two networks and a pace you can keep.
  5. Collect the email addresses of clients who agree to hear from you.
  6. Test ads on a small budget, track them and review your numbers every month.

These marketing mistakes are common, and every one can be fixed. Tackle the biggest gap first and measure what each change brings: a small business with a clear plan and a consistent online presence can grow and stand out.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common marketing mistake small businesses make?
No reliable figure says which one is the most frequent. The one to fix first is marketing without foundations: no website, or one that doesn't say what you do and how to contact you.
How much should a small business spend on marketing?
No figure suits everyone: it depends on your sector, your margins and how many clients your business needs. The basics are affordable: completing your Maps listing is free, and with WebAutonomos a website costs €15/month with no setup fee and no commitment.
Does social media replace a website?
No, they complement each other. On any network you control neither the algorithm nor the rules, and a policy change can cut your reach overnight. A website gives you a space you control, on a domain in your name.
How long does marketing take to bring results?
It depends on the channel. Paid search ads can bring calls from the first day, but you pay for each click. Local SEO and content take longer, often several months, so combining both is a sensible strategy.
Where should I start if I'm making several of these mistakes?
With the basics: a website that says what you do and how to reach you, a complete Google Business Profile and a separate WhatsApp Business number for work. Then add channels gradually and measure the results.
