Which tasks to automate first (and which not to)

Which tasks should you automate first? The ones that come back often, take real time, follow fixed steps, cause errors when done by hand and use data that is already digital. Many business owners begin instead with the task that annoys them most, such as keying in invoices, yet it is not always the one that pays back best. This guide gives you a simple method to rank your repetitive tasks for automation, with examples by trade in Spain.

Should you start with the task you dislike most?

Not necessarily. How much a task annoys you measures friction, not cost: a tedious job done once a week can weigh less than a dull two-minute task repeated twenty times a day.

Other tasks feel light because they belong to the daily routine, yet eat hours over a year: copying new leads into a spreadsheet, retyping customer orders, having to send every new client the same directions.

So the smarter first filter is to count, not to feel: for two weeks, list every repetitive task you and your team notice, with how often it happens and how long it takes.

Five criteria to decide which tasks to automate

Task automation, or process automation when several steps are chained together, means letting software carry out a task automatically whenever a set event happens: a form arrives, an invoice falls due.

With limited resources, the key question is not whether a task can be automated, but whether automating it first makes sense. Check each candidate against five criteria:

  • Frequency: how often it comes back.
  • Time: how long each occurrence takes, interruptions included.
  • Fixed rules: whether the steps are the same, whoever does them.
  • Errors: what manual slips cost today, and what an automated mistake would cost.
  • Data: whether the information the task needs is already digital.

The first two tell you how much time is at stake; the other three, whether the automation will hold up.

Frequency and time: the hours at stake

Count occurrences over a normal week rather than from memory: your sent emails, your invoicing or management software and your WhatsApp chats keep a record.

Then time a few occurrences, from opening the program to getting back to paid work: those minutes are what the automation can give back.

Fixed rules: would two people do it the same way?

Automation applies instructions of the "if this happens, do that" kind. Take ten recent cases: if two people in your team would handle them identically, the work follows a routine you can automate; if each one calls for human judgement, it is not ready yet.

A mix is common: the workflow handles the routine and passes the exceptions to a human. Larger companies often have IT teams to map this; in a small firm, whoever does the task every week knows it best.

Errors: manual slips and automated mistakes

Manual data entry produces typos, missed follow-ups and late invoices, and each one takes time to put right: those errors count in favour of automating, for accuracy as much as efficiency.

Then ask what happens if the automated workflow gets it wrong, because it repeats a mistake as quickly as the right answer. A confirmation with a typo, for example, is minor; a customer invoice with the wrong amount is serious. The higher the risk, the more human review you need at first.

Data: where the information sits

The same task can be simple or complex depending on where its data lives:

  • In apps or a tidy spreadsheet: almost anything is possible, as automation tools can connect apps and update records without retyping.
  • In emails, PDFs or photos: the content has to be read first, often by AI tools, which adds cost and the occasional error.
  • On paper: it has to be digitised first, at least the part the task uses.

How much does a repetitive task really cost?

Multiply how often it happens by how long it takes, then by the cost of an hour of whoever does it.

Ten minutes a day comes to around forty hours over a working year. At €25 an hour, that is €1,000: hard to justify a custom build, because keeping it running can swallow the saving within months.

Two hours a day add up to about 480 hours and around €12,000 a year: the same technical work, a case twelve times stronger. If three people in your team repeat the task, multiply again.

Those hours are what productivity means in a small firm: time you free up for clients, paid work or rest. For the complete calculation, use the formula for the cost of an automation; see also what admin time costs a small business in Spain.

How to score and rank automation candidates

With your list in front of you, ranking takes five steps and no formula:

  1. Go through the recurring tasks and group related ones that belong to the same chain (quote, confirmation, invoice).
  2. Note how often each one happens and how long it takes.
  3. Rate each criterion as High, Medium or Low.
  4. Set aside any task without a fixed routine or with a high risk if it goes wrong.
  5. Rank the rest by hours at stake and pick the top one.

The top row is your first automation project, and you may find it is not the task you had in mind. If none is a good candidate yet, tidy the records of the most promising one first.

Three traps skew the ranking:

  • choosing by annoyance without counting first;
  • picking tools, or AI, before understanding the process;
  • automating a task that each person in your team does differently.

Other pitfalls are covered in the costliest automation mistakes.

Your list also has a blind spot: what you never do. Chasing unpaid invoices, contacting former customers, marketing tasks such as blog articles you could schedule: they cost no hours because they hardly happen, but they cost revenue, a gap explained in what you never get round to.

Which tasks to automate first, trade by trade

The same criteria give different answers in different trades; these common candidates are a starting point, to check against how you work.

Plumbers, electricians and other trades

A plumber in Elche or an electrician in Alicante spends the week on the move, so admin work waits for the evening. Strong candidates:

  • a quote template with your usual items, where an AI assistant can help turn site notes into a draft you check;
  • an away message on WhatsApp Business saying when you will call back;
  • the invoice, sent automatically when a job is marked as finished, then payment reminders;
  • a request for a Google review a few days after the job.

Creating the template may take an evening; the price of each job and the first call with a new client stay with you.

Clinics, therapists and salons

For a physiotherapist in Valencia or a hairdresser in Elda, appointment scheduling drives the week and interrupts the team. A booking link, sent in reply to every "when can you see me?" message, cuts the back-and-forth, and many scheduling tools send their own reminders: for many practices, it is the first automation worth setting up.

Health details need extra care: the GDPR protects them more strictly, so check where each tool stores customer data. Our guide to online booking systems compares the options.

Shops and online sellers

A shop in Alicante can send order confirmations automatically, receive low-stock alerts and pass each order to its stock management software, natively or through Zapier. Repetitive tasks like data entry are a common first automation project: a badly copied order turns into a return.

Customer service replies to frequent enquiries can be saved as templates, and AI tools can help create marketing content such as product descriptions or social media posts to schedule.

Consultants, gestorías and remote freelancers

Office-based professionals repeat other tasks: client onboarding (welcome email, document checklist, engagement letter), document management, recurring invoices and weekly reporting. A gestoría (accountancy and admin office) in Valencia receives many documents as emails and PDFs, where AI can extract the figures for a human to check.

Translators or designers with clients abroad can automate time tracking, updates in their project management tool and the follow-up of unanswered quotes. Small teams also gain from automating the onboarding of new team members, and tools like Trello, Asana or monday.com include task management automations, for example to move a card to the next stage.

Quick automation wins with the tools you already pay for

Before buying new tools or automation software, check the features built into the apps you already use, often the quickest way to get started:

  • repeat invoices and payment reminders in your invoicing app, which has to comply with Spain's invoicing rules;
  • greeting and away messages, plus quick replies, in WhatsApp Business;
  • saved replies for the enquiries you answer every week: create them once, and let AI help with the first drafts;
  • a welcome email sent automatically by your email marketing service;
  • posts you schedule in advance on social media;
  • contact form entries and new leads copied automatically into Google Sheets or your CRM (customer relationship management) software, if not natively then with a basic Zapier workflow.

None of this requires a developer: it is workflow automation at its simplest, and it can free up time quickly.

Which automation tools fit which tasks?

Automation software comes in three families: tools built into your apps, a no-code platform that links them, and artificial intelligence (AI).

Many processes in a small business need no AI at all: if an order arrives, the invoice goes out automatically; if a payment is overdue, a reminder is sent. AI earns its place when the task means reading or writing text that varies.

Connecting apps without code

To automate tasks that cross several apps, no-code automation tools like Zapier or Make connect them: a trigger in one app (a form is submitted, an email arrives) launches actions in others. Zapier calls these workflows Zaps; small teams can build their first ones from ready-made templates.

These workflows work best for frequent tasks with a clear trigger, and our guide to no-code automation shows how far you can go without outside help.

AI automation: when is it worth it?

AI tools can read a supplier invoice in PDF, sort incoming customer enquiries or draft an email reply for you to check. They make occasional mistakes, so let AI suggest and keep a human review on anything that reaches a client or your accounts.

If you can describe the job with fixed conditions, start without AI: fewer moving parts to maintain. If someone offers you an AI agent that decides alone, ask what happens when the AI gets it wrong. See our selection of AI options for freelancers.

Robotic process automation and custom builds

For older systems with no link to other apps, robotic process automation (RPA) uses software robots that click and type like a person. Custom development handles complex processes and work no ready-made tools cover. Both need more time and resources, so keep them for large volumes.

What to leave off your list for now

Not every repetitive task makes a strong first project. At the start, set aside:

  • the first reply to a new client and any complaint, best kept personal because tone matters;
  • decisions that call for judgement: a special price, a discount, an exception;
  • jobs that happen a few times a year;
  • processes you do not yet carry out consistently;
  • replies written by AI and sent unread;
  • anything with legal or tax consequences, unless you check the result yourself.

Some of these should never be automated, as explained in what not to automate in business; others wait until your first automation runs smoothly.

And if a task takes hours every week but still calls for judgement, the question may be whether to hire or automate.

From your ranking to a first automation project

Keep your first workflow automation small: one task, one trigger, one person in your team responsible for checking it. Measure the starting point (time spent, errors, response times), write down the process as it is really done and choose the right tool, often among the tools you already have.

Let the automation run alongside the manual process for a few weeks, with an alert if it stops, then compare with the starting point to see whether the productivity and efficiency gains are real. Check any AI step closely during those weeks. Each stage is described in how automating a process works, step by step.

Count the cost of maintenance too: every automation has to be updated when one of your apps changes. Once the first workflows have started paying off, score the next task on your list.

Frequently asked questions

Which task should I automate first if I have no idea where to start?
The one that takes the most time in total, provided it is always done the same way and its data is already digital. To find it, note for a week or two how often each repetitive task happens and how long it takes. It is often a short, frequent task rather than the one that annoys you most.
Should I automate everything at once?
No. Several automations launched together multiply the points of failure, and none of them settles. Start with one automation, measure the result over a few weeks, and choose the next one with what you have learned.
Do I need AI to automate tasks in a small business?
Not always. Many automations work with simple conditions, such as sending a reminder when an invoice falls due. AI can help when the task involves reading free text or documents in varying formats, such as supplier invoices, and a person should check what the AI produces.
What if the task keeps changing its rules?
Then it is better to wait. Automation is built on stable rules; if they change every quarter, maintenance eats the saving. Improve and stabilise the process first, before automating it.
How much does it cost to automate a task?
There is no single figure: it depends on the number of steps, where the data sits and the technology needed. Begin by working out what the manual task costs you today: times per week, multiplied by minutes and by the cost of an hour. That figure tells you how much it makes sense to spend.
