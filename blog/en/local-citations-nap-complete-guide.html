Local citations and NAP: what they are and how to build them in Spain

Local citations are online mentions of your business name, address and phone number on sites other than your own: directories, social media profiles, associations or the regional press. Search engines like Google can use them to confirm that your business exists and that your details are accurate, and potential customers use them to find you. This guide is for freelancers and small and medium-sized businesses in Spain: what citations are, why your NAP must match everywhere, which directories are useful and how to audit your citations, with examples from Valencia, Alicante, Elche and Elda.

What are local citations?

A local citation is any online mention that includes your business name together with its address, its phone number or both, on a site that isn't yours. Your profile on Páginas Amarillas, your Instagram account and a story in Levante-EMV in which your shop and its street are mentioned are all citations.

In SEO terms, citations are external signals: they live outside your own pages, like backlinks and reviews. When several independent sites show identical details, they confirm one another; when they disagree, trust drops.

What NAP stands for

NAP stands for name, address and phone number, the three details that identify a local business: who it is, its location and how to reach it.

Many guides add a W for website (NAP+W), and profiles on directories often include opening hours, categories, photos and a description. The NAP is the core, though: the part that should be identical from one source to the next.

Structured and unstructured citations

There are two types of local citations, and it's best to have both:

  • Structured citations: your details in fixed fields on directories and platforms, such as Google Business Profile, Páginas Amarillas, QDQ, Yelp or Facebook. You fill them in yourself, so you control them.
  • Unstructured citations: mentions inside a text, such as a newspaper article, a supplier's list of recommended installers or a report on an event you sponsor in your town. You don't control the wording, but they can bring authority when they come from relevant local sources.

Structured citations are easy for a search engine to read, so focus on them first. Unstructured ones take longer and come from your relationships in the area: your town hall, your trade association, local media and the businesses you work with.

Don't confuse citations with backlinks, which are clickable links from other sites to yours: a profile without one is still a citation. Our guide to local link building explains how to earn them in your area.

Why are local citations important for local SEO?

Citations help search engines confirm who you are, where you are and how to contact you, and they put your local business in front of potential customers on the platforms they use. Accurate citations support your position in local search results; inconsistent ones confuse search engines and people alike.

What Google says about local search results

Google explains that its local results are mainly based on relevance (how well a profile matches what someone is looking for), distance (how far the business is from the person searching) and prominence (how well known the business is).

It adds that prominence also relies on information such as how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have, and that businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to show up in local search results.

Google's help pages don't say how much each signal weighs in the ranking. Citations fit into this picture because profiles on directories, press mentions and association listings are all sources of data about you, and each citation that repeats your details helps confirm them.

In competitive sectors, such signals can help you rank in the Map Pack, the block of local search results shown with a map.

Consistent details build trust

Search engines gather data about each local business from many sources. If your information changes from one platform to another, they may not know which version is right, or may treat the variants as separate businesses. For SEO, the goal is simple: one version of your details everywhere.

That consistency matters for people too: someone who finds an old number and gets no answer may well call a competitor instead. Consistent and accurate business listings help people contact you directly and bring referral traffic from review sites like Yelp.

AI assistants that answer questions such as "which electrician can come today in Elche?" may also draw on this online information, so outdated details could end up in their answers.

Are local citations still important in 2026?

Yes, but they're only part of the picture. Many SEO experts believe citations would now weigh less in local search than reviews, proximity or a complete Business Profile, and see them as a foundation rather than a way to rank above competitors.

Without consistent citations, though, the rest of your local SEO strategy rests on shaky ground and your online presence sends mixed signals.

Directories and platforms useful in Spain

You don't need local citations on every directory that exists. A good SEO strategy is to cover three types of platforms in order: start with the map platforms, then the general business directories, then those specific to your trade and your town. A few complete, quality profiles are worth more than hundreds of abandoned ones.

Map platforms and search engines

These listings appear in local search, when people look for a local business on a map or a search engine, often from their mobile:

  • Google Business Profile: your listing in Google's search results and on its map, and the reference for all your other citations. Our guide on how to add your business to Google Maps explains how to create and verify it.
  • Bing Places for Business: Microsoft's equivalent for Bing and Bing Maps. You can import your information from Google, which keeps both consistent.
  • Apple Business: Apple's service for companies, where you can claim your location on Apple Maps and update its information.

General business directories

These directories accept companies from almost any sector:

Check the quality of each directory before you get listed: if it's full of outdated entries or spam, or isn't relevant to your sector, skip it. A directory that nobody visits, and that never appears in search results, brings no traffic either.

Directories for your trade or industry

Industry platforms are a great source of relevant citations, and the traffic they send comes from an audience already looking for your kind of service.

Doctoralia lists health professionals; TripAdvisor covers restaurants, accommodation and activities, with traveller reviews; your colegio profesional or trade association may publish a members' guide; and in home renovation and the trades, look for the sites that feature professionals in your field.

Your suppliers and colleagues can help you find where they're listed and what works best in your sector.

Local sources and social networks

Some town halls, chambers of commerce and business associations publish directories of local businesses: these citations come from sources people trust, so look for the one in Valencia, Alicante, Elche, Elda or your own town. Mentions in local media such as Las Provincias or Información are unstructured citations that carry authority in your area.

Your Facebook page, Instagram profile and LinkedIn company profile also count as citations that search engines can find, so show the same details on them as everywhere else. To choose your networks, see our guide to social media for the self-employed.

How to build local citations step by step

Building citations is careful, repetitive SEO work, and the strategy is simple: define your details once and copy them exactly, so they stay consistent on each platform.

  1. Write down your official NAP and the rest of your data in a document, and copy from it for all your citations.
  2. Start with your Google Business Profile and your website: every other citation will be compared with them.
  3. Claim or create your profiles on Bing Places and Apple Maps.
  4. Build citations on the general directories, then on those for your trade and your town.
  5. Complete each profile with the same category, services, opening hours, description and photos.
  6. Verify each listing by phone, email or post when the directory offers it, and record it in a spreadsheet.

Define your official NAP

Consistency here is critical, because small variations accumulate. In Spain, watch out for these details:

  • Name: use the trade name people know you by, not a mix of your legal name, the "S.L." version and an abbreviation. Don't add keywords or towns to your business name to help your SEO: Google's guidelines ask for the name you use in the real world.
  • Address: pick one format (Calle or C/, Avenida or Avda.), include the floor or door if needed and always the postcode. In Valencian-speaking towns, a street can appear as Carrer or Calle: choose one and use it everywhere.
  • Phone: choose one main number, landline or mobile. A mobile that also receives WhatsApp messages is a practical choice for many freelancers.

Complete every profile

A bare profile does little for customers or search engines, and improving it is quick. Fill in as much information as each platform allows: the most specific category that fits (the same as on Google, or the closest equivalent), your services, opening hours and a short description, plus a few photos. If the platform shows reviews, reply to them as well.

Write one description and adapt its length to each platform instead of inventing a new text every time. For help with categories, see choosing your Business Profile categories.

Doing it yourself or paying for a service

You can build citations manually: many directories offer free registration, and it mainly takes patience. If you delegate citation building to an SEO agency, ask for the list of sites where your citations are published, and for the logins, to ensure the listings stay yours.

Citation strategies designed for the UK or the US often rely on data aggregators: companies that collect business data and pass it on to directories and apps (Foursquare is one example). Before paying for such a service, check which Spanish directories these aggregators actually reach.

How to audit and fix your citations

A citation audit, a routine part of local SEO, means finding every place where your business is mentioned and correcting the citations that are wrong, outdated or duplicated. It's particularly important if your business has moved or had a change of name, because old details may still be online.

Find every citation

Look up each of these in Google and Bing, in quotation marks:

  • your business name, alone and followed by your town;
  • your phone number, with and without spaces;
  • your current address and any previous one;
  • any former phone or business name.

Check the search results on your mobile too, as a customer would, and record every citation you've found in your spreadsheet.

Correct, merge or remove

  • If the listing is yours, log in or claim it, then update the information.
  • If a directory shows your business twice, ask it to merge or remove the duplicate.
  • For unstructured mentions, such as an old article, write to the publisher and ask politely for a correction.
  • Correct your Google profile and your own site first, to ensure every other fix points to the same reference.

Free spreadsheet or paid tools?

Paid SEO tools such as BrightLocal can find and track your business listings automatically, which can help if you manage several locations; check that a tool covers Spanish directories before subscribing.

For a freelancer with a handful of profiles, a spreadsheet is usually enough to keep everything consistent: one row per citation, with its URL, the login email, the data published and the date checked. Review it every six months and whenever your NAP is updated.

NAP errors to avoid

These mistakes are easy to make. Each one is a potential source of confusion for search engines, can weaken your local presence and can make it harder to rank in local search:

  • Using different names across your citations: the trade name on one platform, the legal name on another, an abbreviation on a third.
  • Mixing landline and mobile numbers.
  • Writing the address in a new format each time, or leaving out the postcode.
  • Being listed twice on one platform.
  • Leaving old citations online after a move.
  • Pointing citations to a URL that no longer works.
  • Leaving profiles half finished, with no category, hours or photos.

Service-area businesses and moving premises

Electricians, plumbers, cleaners and many other trades work at their clients' homes rather than at their premises. Google lets these service-area businesses hide their address and show the areas they serve.

Apply the same choice everywhere: if it's hidden on your Google profile, keep it off directories too; give your town and service area, with the same name and contact details.

If you move or get a new phone line, update your Google Business Profile first, then every other citation in your spreadsheet, from the other map platforms to the smallest directory. A few weeks later, look for the old details again and check the results to catch any listing you missed.

The two citations you control completely

Two citations are particularly important, because all your other citations should match them.

Your Google Business Profile is a highly visible citation, shown on Google Maps and in local search results. Keep every section complete and keep improving it: see our guides to optimising your Business Profile and getting more reviews.

Your website is a citation too: include your business name, address and phone number in full in the footer and on the contact page, exactly as on your profile, so visitors can find them and call you directly.

LocalBusiness markup, code that visitors don't see, labels each piece of information for search engines, helping them read it correctly: our guide to Schema LocalBusiness explains it.

Examples in Valencia, Alicante, Elche and Elda

Four typical citation problems, and how to sort them out:

  • A hairdresser in Valencia is listed with the salon's landline on one directory and a mobile on another: the salon picks one number and updates every listing.
  • An electrician in Alicante who works at clients' homes hides the street on Google and shows the service area; their other listings give only the town and the same mobile.
  • A car repair workshop in Elda moves to a new location in an industrial estate, but an old directory still shows the previous street and a customer turns up at an empty unit. The listing is found when the owner looks up the old street, and the workshop has it corrected.
  • A shoe shop in Elche appears as "Carrer" on Yelp and "Calle" on Páginas Amarillas, with its legal name on another profile. It aligns every citation with the street name and trade name on its Google profile.

Local citations are one piece of your local SEO strategy, alongside your Google Business Profile, your reviews and your website. What matters isn't being on as many sites as possible but showing exactly the same NAP on all of them. Begin with the platforms that matter in your sector, complete each profile and check your citations regularly.

Frequently asked questions

What does NAP mean in local SEO?
NAP stands for name, address and phone number, the three basic details of a local business. They should be identical on your website, your Google Business Profile, directories such as Páginas Amarillas and your social media profiles, such as Facebook, so that search engines and customers can trust them.
How many local citations does my business need?
There's no magic number: the quality and consistency of your citations matter more than their quantity. Begin with the map platforms and the general directories, then those for your trade and your town, and stop when you no longer find relevant sites.
What happens if my NAP is not consistent across platforms?
If search engines find different details about you on different platforms, such as one number on Páginas Amarillas and another on Yelp, they may be less sure which information is right, and your local ranking can suffer. Customers may also call a line that no longer works.
Do local citations still matter in 2026?
Yes. They remain a signal that your business is real and present online. Many SEO experts believe they would now weigh less in local search than reviews, distance or a complete Business Profile, so treat them as a foundation for the rest of your local SEO.
Can I create local citations myself, or do I need a professional?
You can do it yourself: many directories offer free registration, and you only need to create an account, fill in your business details and verify the listing. If you hire an SEO agency, ask for the list of sites where it publishes your details and check that they match your official details.
What is the difference between a local citation and a backlink?
A local citation mentions your business name and contact details on another platform, with or without a link. A backlink is a clickable link from another site to yours, so a listing that links to you counts as both.
Do I need a physical address to have local citations?
No. If you work at your customers' homes, Google lets you hide your address and show your service area instead. On directories, use the same name, number and town, and don't publish an address you've hidden elsewhere.
How often should I check my local citations?
Every six months, and each time any part of your NAP changes. Look up your business name, phone and any old details in quotation marks: the results show which listings still need updating.
