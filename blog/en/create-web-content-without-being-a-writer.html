You don't need to be a writer to create good website content. You need to know your customers, what they want to find out and how to say it in plain language. This guide, for freelancers and small business owners in Spain who keep putting off the job, covers how to plan your pages, what each page should say, how online writing differs and how to get it done in an hour a week.

What makes good website content?

Good website content tells the right people what you do, where you do it and how to reach you, quickly and in terms they understand. It's written for your customers, not for you, and it's easy to read on a phone. Good content is:

  • Useful: it answers the questions people ask you every week.
  • Specific: it names your services, your area and how you price a job.
  • Easy to scan: short paragraphs, titles and lists.
  • Easy to act on: every section leads to the next step.
  • Yours: it sounds like you, not like a template copied from another site.

Start with your readers and the questions they ask

Before you write anything, picture the people who will visit your site, such as a family in Alicante with a leaking roof. Then write down the questions customers ask you on the phone or on WhatsApp: How much will it cost? Do you cover my area? Do you speak English? Those questions are your content plan, and the answers are the information to publish. Next, do a little research into the words they use:

  • Type your service and town into Google and note the suggestions (see how to choose local keywords).
  • Read your reviews and messages: customers often describe the problem (“the boiler keeps cutting out”), not the trade terms.

Plan your pages before you start writing

Keep it simple: five types of page are enough to start.

  1. Homepage: what you do, for whom and where.
  2. A page for each main service, or one with a section for each.
  3. About page: who you are and why people can trust you.
  4. Contact page: every way to reach you, your hours and the areas you cover.
  5. Legal texts: a legal notice, a privacy policy and a cookie policy, which a business website in Spain needs.

For each one, note who it's for, which question it answers and what the reader should do next. That plan gives your site a simple structure, and the writing gets much easier. Our checklist of the elements every freelancer's website must have shows what might be missing.

What should each page say?

Each page should answer one main question and lead to one next step.

Homepage: what you do, for whom and where

Your homepage must show visitors at once that they're in the right place. Compare “Welcome to our website” with “Emergency plumber in Elche: leaks, blocked drains and boilers”. Below the headline, include a brief summary of each service with links to the details, proof to support what you say, such as photos of your jobs or your Google reviews, and a button to call or send a WhatsApp message. A clean design helps, but your content does the selling.

Service pages: the problem, the result and how you work

To plan each service description, use a simple three-part formula:

  1. The problem: your customer's situation, as they would describe it.
  2. The result: what changes once the job is done. Talk about benefits, not just features: “hot water again today” means more than “boiler repair”.
  3. How you work: the steps, how long it takes and what's included.

Then add prices where possible, or explain how you quote.

About page: why people can trust you

Visitors go there to decide whether to trust you with the job. Tell them who you are, your experience, your qualifications and the languages you speak. A photo of you adds a human touch, and it's part of your personal brand.

Contact page: make it easy to reach you

Include your phone number, a WhatsApp button (see how to set up WhatsApp Business), a simple form, the towns you cover and your working hours.

How is writing for the web different?

Online, many users scan for the information they need instead of reading every word, often on a phone. With good web content, that information is easy to find and quick to take in.

Put the essential information first

Start each section with the answer, then add the details. Someone who stops reading after the first lines should still know what you offer and where. Visitors often land straight on an inner page from Google, so it should make sense on its own.

Keep sentences and paragraphs short

Long sentences are hard to follow on a phone. Aim for one idea per sentence and one topic per paragraph, and break up any block longer than a few lines. Use the active voice (“we install boilers”, not “boilers are installed”) and talk to the reader as “you”. Short text isn't lazy: it's easier to read.

Use headings, subheadings and bullet points

Headings act as signposts: they let readers easily find the part that interests them and help Google understand how your content is organised. Use one main heading, then section titles phrased the way a customer would ask (for example, “How much does a boiler service cost?”). When you list things, use bullet points. Keep the formatting as simple as possible.

Choose plain language over jargon

Write as you would explain the job to a customer in front of you, in a conversational style. Avoid specialist vocabulary; if you need a technical term, explain it. Simple wording is quicker for everyone, experts included.

Instead of Try
“We are dedicated to providing comprehensive solutions” “We fix leaks, blocked drains and boilers”
“Do not hesitate to get in touch” “Call or WhatsApp us for a quote”
“We are a company committed to quality and customer satisfaction” “I'm a plumber in Elche with 12 years' experience, and I speak English and Spanish”

Vague, general claims don't build trust, because any business could make them; specific details do. And if many of your customers are British or Irish, UK English (colour, organise) will feel natural.

Help search engines understand your content

Google analyses your content to decide which searches it answers: that is how new customers find you. You don't need complex keyword research: three habits cover the basics.

  • Use the words people type. Name your service and town in the title, the main heading and the first lines: “electrician in Valencia”, not just “electrical services”. Mention the terms your customers use where they fit naturally, and avoid repeating them in every line.
  • Give each page its own title and description. The title is the headline users click in Google's results, for example “Emergency plumber in Elche | Your business name”; the meta description is the summary that may appear below it.
  • Add internal links. Link from your homepage to each service and from articles to the service they mention, with link text that says where it leads (“boiler repairs in Elche”) rather than “click here”.

Remember that you're writing for humans first: a search engine is just one more reader. Every website we build includes basic SEO, with titles and texts that mention your service and area. To go further, see how to rank your website on Google as a local business.

Make your content accessible

Content that everyone can use, including anyone who relies on a screen reader (software that reads the screen aloud), is better for every user. A few simple habits:

  • Describe your images in their alt text (“plumber repairing a boiler in Elche”), so screen readers and Google know what they show.
  • Keep titles in a logical order, so users can jump between sections.
  • Make links say where they go.
  • Don't put key information, such as prices, only inside images.
  • Make sure the text size is comfortable and the design has enough contrast.

Finish with a clear call to action

Show visitors what to do next, in a short phrase they can act on, such as:

  • “Send us a WhatsApp with a photo of the problem”
  • “Call now for a quote in Alicante”

Put it after each service, at the end of each section and, for mobile users, on a button that stays visible. One main action works best: several options at once can make visitors hesitate.

Dictate first, tidy up later

If content writing feels difficult, talk rather than type. Explaining your job out loud often comes more naturally, and the result sounds like you.

  1. Pick one section and list the questions it must answer.
  2. Open a note on your phone and tap the microphone key: the keyboard on iPhone and Android phones usually includes dictation.
  3. Answer each question out loud, as if a customer had asked it.
  4. Tidy up the transcript: cut repetitions, split sentences that run on and break it into sections.
  5. Read it aloud to ensure it sounds natural.

Use AI as an assistant, not as the author

AI tools such as ChatGPT can help you get started, but they don't know your business, your prices or your town. Use them to polish your draft, not to produce your web content from scratch. They're good for:

  • turning your dictated notes into a first draft with sections;
  • simplifying an explanation;
  • correcting typos and clumsy phrasing.

Content generated from nothing tends to be generic and can contain mistakes or invented details, so verify every fact and rework it in your own style and voice. Your own examples are what make the content interesting. For more ideas, see our guide to AI tools for freelancers.

Proofread your content and keep it up to date

Once you write a first draft, let it rest for a day: clumsy sentences stand out on a second look. Ask a friend who doesn't know your trade for feedback: can they explain what you do and how to reach you? A checker such as LanguageTool can catch spelling and grammar mistakes in English and Spanish. Then look at the whole site on your phone.

When your site is online, keep it current: out-of-date prices or opening hours make visitors doubt everything else. Review your content every few months so that visitors find up-to-date information. Your action plan: one hour a week

Block one hour a week, at a time that suits you best: 30 minutes dictating, 20 minutes editing and 10 minutes publishing.

  1. Week 1: list your readers and their questions.
  2. Week 2: draft your homepage.
  3. Week 3: draft your service pages.
  4. Week 4: finish the about section and the ways to reach you, then proofread.
  5. After that: one new piece of content a week, such as an article or an update.

Keep the habit for six months and you'll have around 24 new articles or updates, each answering one more question, which can help more customers find you online over time.

Frequently asked questions

How much content does a freelancer's website need?
A homepage, a description of each main service, a few lines about you and how to reach you are enough to start, plus your legal texts. The right length is whatever answers your customers' questions.
Can I use ChatGPT for my website content?
Yes, as an assistant rather than as the author. AI can suggest a structure, simplify a draft and spot typos, but it has no idea of your business, your prices or your area, and it can make mistakes. Draft the content yourself, or dictate it, then verify every fact.
What if I really don't like writing?
Try dictation: tap the microphone key on your phone's keyboard, answer your customers' usual questions out loud and tidy up the transcript. If you'd rather delegate, WebAutonomos includes the texts in the sites it builds, prepared from your Google Business Profile and photos.
Should my website be in English, Spanish or both?
