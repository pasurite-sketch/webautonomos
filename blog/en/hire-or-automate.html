Should you hire or automate? How to decide

Sooner or later the admin overflows and the question arrives: should you hire someone, or automate part of the work? If you are self-employed (an autónomo) or run a small company in Spain, it comes back each time your business grows. It is often framed the wrong way: a salary gets compared with a software subscription, and nobody looks at what each option actually does. An employee costs more than the payslip shows; an automation can cost less than people fear, but only covers part of the work. This guide helps you decide whether to hire or automate, block by block, with your own numbers.

When to hire and when to automate: the basic rule

Automate work that is repetitive and follows the same steps every time, and hire people for work that needs judgment, human contact or decisions. In many companies, the right question is not which option to pick but how to split the work: a tool runs the routine workflows and a person takes care of the rest.

To get that split right, look at three things for each part of the work: what it involves, how often it comes up and the real cost of each route.

Hire vs automate: which work suits a tool and which a person

The first filter, before any cost comparison, is the nature of the work your business needs done. Repetitive, predictable processes can be automated; work with exceptions, judgment calls or customer contact needs experienced people. This table shows where to draw the line:

Type of work Examples Best fit
Repetitive and predictable Copying data between systems, sending reminders, filing invoices, updating a spreadsheet Automate
Repetitive, with exceptions Chasing late payments, checking paperwork before it goes to your gestoría Automate the fixed part, keep a person for the exceptions
Reading and sorting text Reading supplier invoices, sorting incoming emails, drafting a first reply An AI tool, with a person checking the result
Judgment or customer contact Negotiating a price, handling a complaint, making a decision on an exception A person, supported by technology

Picture a family-run travel agency in Valencia that receives requests by email, by WhatsApp and through its website form. Copying each request into the bookings spreadsheet and sending payment reminders is repetitive work for a tool; putting together a tailor-made trip takes a person's judgment. The two are different kinds of work: an automation does one thing the same way every time, without tiring, while a person can answer the phone, notice when things look odd and adapt to an unusual request.

How often does the work actually repeat?

Part of the answer depends on volume: the more often a piece of work repeats, the more it makes sense to automate it. If it only comes up a few times a month, doing it by hand may cost less than having it automated, so measure the real volume instead of guessing. A job with low volume, or low value, may not be worth the build.

Volume matters when you hire, too. A team has a limited capacity, so a growing business needs more people or has to free up the time of the ones it has. A well-built system absorbs extra volume without overtime, even in summer for businesses on the Costa Blanca, provided the process stays the same. The real cost of hiring someone in Spain

In Spain, a new hire costs the business more than the salary on the payslip: social security paid by the company, holidays, sick leave, a workstation, recruitment, training and turnover all come on top. Ask your gestoría for the total cost of the role over a full year before you compare it with anything else.

Base salary vs total cost of the role

The base salary is just the starting point of the comparison. Several of these expenses only show up during the first year:

  • Social security: the company pays its own contributions on top of the gross salary.
  • Holidays and sick leave: every year, the work stops for a while or someone has to cover it.
  • The workstation: a desk, a computer, a phone and software licences.
  • Recruitment: the job advert, the interviews and the choice take time away from your customers.
  • Training and management: until the new hire knows your processes, their productivity is lower than their cost, and someone in your team has to explain and check.
  • Turnover: if the person leaves, you start again, and what they had learnt goes with them.

In return, people bring what no software offers: judgment, initiative, human contact and the ability to deal with the unexpected. The relationships someone builds with your customers and suppliers over the years are hard to hand over to a tool.

The paperwork of a first hire

If you have no employees yet, your first hire also creates admin: you must register as an employer with the Social Security system, register the employee before their first day and issue monthly payslips. Many companies leave the contract and the payroll processing to a gestoría, which can also tell you which collective agreement (convenio colectivo) applies to your sector: the agreement can set minimum wages and working hours.

What it costs to automate a process

Automation costs money too, but in a different way: much of the spend comes at the start, with smaller ongoing expenses afterwards. Take into account the one-time build (studying the process, setting up the automation and testing it with real documents), the subscription to the software it runs on, maintenance when a supplier changes its invoice layout or your process changes, and your own time to explain the work and check the first results.

What gets expensive is often not the tools themselves but how you build them: on top of a disorganised process, you end up paying for something that does not work. Before you accept a proposal, ask for a breakdown of maintenance, line by line. For the full method, read how to work out the cost to automate a process and why maintenance is the hidden cost of automation.

Existing tools vs a custom build

Some of this work does not need enterprise systems: it can be covered by software you already pay for, such as email filters, your invoicing program or the integrations between apps your company uses. A custom build makes sense when the work runs across several systems that do not talk to each other, or when no existing tool is the right fit for your processes. Before paying for new automation tools, see how far no-code automation can take you.

Where does AI fit in the decision?

AI widens what you can automate, because it can read and write ordinary text instead of only following fixed rules. It still makes mistakes, so people need to check its work before it reaches a customer or the tax office.

AI and automation are not quite the same thing. Classic automation follows instructions set in advance: when an email arrives with an attachment, save it in a folder. A tool built on an AI model interprets content: it can tell an invoice from a quote or work out what a customer is asking for.

What an AI tool can take on

  • Reading a receipt or a supplier's bill and extracting the date and the amount for your bookkeeping.
  • Sorting incoming emails: quote requests, complaints, invoices, spam.
  • Drafting a reply to a frequent question, for you to check and send.
  • Summarising a long email thread or a document.

What AI cannot do on its own

AI can sound sure of itself and still be wrong, for example by misreading a figure or inventing a detail. It does not know your customers, takes no responsibility and does not notice when a case is an exception.

If you give it client data, data protection rules (the GDPR) apply to that processing, so read the privacy terms and check which companies can access the information. Let AI prepare the work and leave the decision to a human.

Do you need an AI agent or a custom AI?

An AI agent chains several actions on its own: it reads a request, looks up information and prepares an answer or the next step. That can save time, but the more it does unsupervised, the further a single mistake can travel. For a small business, a tool that does one clear thing is easier to check than a custom AI agent that tries to run all your workflows. For examples, see our selection of AI tools for freelancers.

Timescales and risks of each option

Neither option is instant. Hiring takes recruitment and onboarding before the new hire is up to speed; automating takes time to study the process, build, test and adjust. Whichever you choose, set a date to check whether the decision is actually working for your business.

The risks are different, too. With people, turnover and sick leave can leave a gap, and the know-how can end up in a single head. With a tool, a change in regulations, an error nobody notices, a free plan whose conditions change or a breach involving your customers' data can leave the system useless. Document your workflows to reduce both risks: a documented automation can run for years if the process does not change.

The middle ground: part-time work and a hybrid model

Often the answer is a mix: a tool takes the repetitive part, with a person handling the rest. That person can be you as the business owner, a member of your team or someone you hire part-time, now free to look after customers, deal with exceptions and check what the automation does.

In a hybrid model, decide from the start who owns the automation: who notices when it stops, reads the error message and updates it when something changes.

Part-time vs full-time: is half a role enough?

A part-time contract (contrato a tiempo parcial) for half the hours costs roughly half as much in salary and social security and keeps the human flexibility, although recruitment and training cost about the same. It becomes questionable when a good share of those hours goes into work a machine would do without errors.

Signs that point to automating or to hiring

Signs that automation wins

  • You do the same work several times a week, in the same order.
  • You copy information by hand from one system to another.
  • The volume keeps growing and the backlog builds up.
  • Errors come from tiredness, not from a lack of knowledge.

Signs that hiring wins

  • The work changes every time and needs judgment.
  • Personal contact is what sets your company apart, as it does for many service businesses.
  • The procedure changes often, so a tool would soon be out of date.
  • Customer relationships suffer because nobody has time to look after them.

Common mistakes when deciding

A frequent mistake is to compare prices only: a salary against a subscription. That comparison falls apart as soon as you forget what it costs to maintain the tool or to supervise the new hire. The question to ask is what each option does, not only its cost.

Other mistakes are automating a process that is not yet in order, taking on people to cover up an organisation or management problem, leaving the automation without an owner, and automating a task whose rules change every quarter: it is a poor candidate, because the build would need redoing each time. For more on these traps, read about the costliest automation mistakes and what you should never automate.

How to make the decision in six steps

Before you hire, work through this list:

  1. Write down exactly what the person you hire would actually do, and split it into blocks.
  2. For each block, note the number of times it comes up per day or per week and how long it takes.
  3. Mark whether it needs judgment or only execution.
  4. Set aside the blocks that repeat without variation: these are the ones you could automate.
  5. Ask your gestoría for the total cost of the role, full-time and part-time, not just the salary.
  6. Price the automation of those blocks, taking maintenance into account, and compare what is left with the cost of a hire.

You will often find that half of the work is repetitive. Automate it, and with the hours you free up, the other half may no longer justify a full-time role. To see how a project unfolds, read how automating a process works, step by step.

Frequently asked questions

What does it really cost to hire an employee in Spain?
In Spain, an employee costs the business more than the gross salary: social security contributions, paid holidays, sick leave, a workstation, recruitment, training and the risk of turnover come on top. Ask your gestoría for the total cost of the role, based on your own numbers.
Does automation replace a job?
Rarely in full. Automation takes over the repetitive part of a job and leaves the exceptions, the checks and everything that needs judgment to a person. The useful question is how to split the work, not which option to choose.
Is a part-time hire better than a full-time one?
It can be. A part-time contract for half the hours costs roughly half as much in salary and social security and keeps the human flexibility, although recruitment and training cost about the same. It makes less sense if much of that time goes into work a tool could do.
What if I automate and then my business changes?
That is a real risk. An automation needs a stable process, so work whose rules change every quarter is a poor candidate. If your organisation is still changing, put it in order first and automate once things have settled.
What does it cost to automate a task?
It depends on the price of doing the work by hand today, the number of programs involved and what the automation costs to build and maintain. No figure fits all businesses: the free diagnostic on webautonomos.es estimates yours in about two minutes.
When is it better to hire than to automate?
It is better to hire when the work needs judgment or close contact with customers, or when the procedure changes often, because a tool would perform poorly there. If the work also comes up only a few times a month, an automation may not pay for itself.
Can AI do the work of an administrative assistant?
Only part of it. AI tools can read invoices, sort emails and draft replies, but they make mistakes and take no decisions or responsibility. In practice, AI can prepare part of the routine admin in a business, while a person checks the results and deals with customers.
