When a dog starts limping or a cat stops eating, many owners grab their phone and search for a vet nearby. Good veterinary website design gives them the answers they need in seconds. This guide explains what to put on a clinic website in Spain, with examples from Valencia, Alicante and Elche.

Why does a veterinary clinic need a website?

A veterinary clinic needs a website because many pet owners look for a clinic online before they call, and your website is the one place where you decide what they see. Social media is useful, but it follows someone else's rules.

What pet owners want to see first

  • which services you offer and which animals you treat;
  • where you are and how to get there;
  • when you open and what happens in an emergency;
  • who will care for their pet, and what other clients say.

Emergency contact: when every minute counts

In an emergency, a worried owner won't fill in a long form: they want to call, or to know straight away whether you're open.

Phone number, hours and out-of-hours care

Put your number at the top of every page as a link that calls you with one tap, and your up-to-date hours just below it, including public holidays. If you don't see animals out of hours, say so and tell owners where to go instead; if you take emergencies, explain which number to call and whether to ring first.

Non-urgent visits: a booking link, a call or WhatsApp

For routine visits, give owners a choice: a link or button to the online booking tool you already use, a phone call, WhatsApp or a form that sends each message to your email. What pages should a veterinary website include?

A veterinary website needs six essential sections, and a small practice doesn't need dozens of pages:

  • Home: who you are, where you are, your hours and a button to call.
  • Services: what you do and which animals you treat.
  • Vets and staff: photos, roles and training.
  • Pet care advice: short articles for owners.
  • Location: address, map, parking and payment methods.
  • Legal texts: legal notice, privacy policy and cookie policy.

Veterinary services described in plain words

Create a short section for each main service, explain what happens during the visit so people learn what to expect, and describe what you do rather than what you will achieve. List only what you offer:

  • consultations and vaccination;
  • surgery and neutering;
  • dentistry and diagnostic tests;
  • emergencies or grooming, if you provide them.

Pet care content

Keep the articles short and practical, on topics such as the vaccination calendar or keeping pets cool in a Valencia summer: they show your knowledge and can help you appear in search results. Present them as general information, because an article doesn't replace an examination, and add fresh content from time to time.

Design: what clients expect from a veterinary website

Good web design helps visitors find the key information quickly: a calm, clear layout, your own photos and one obvious call to action on each page, such as “Call now”, “Book an appointment” or “Message us on WhatsApp”.

Branding, images and videos

Use the colours and logo from your sign and uniforms, so the site matches the practice people visit; whether you choose a template or a custom design, two or three colours with strong contrast are enough. Show images of your reception, consultation rooms and staff rather than stock photos; a short video tour can help nervous owners feel at ease. Built for mobile

Many clients will look you up on their mobile. Check that your number can be tapped to call and that your site loads quickly: speed also counts for Google, as our article on website speed and search rankings explains.

Veterinary website examples: what to show first

What works best on the first screen depends on the clinic:

  • A neighbourhood clinic in Elche: hours, phone number and map at the top, then vaccinations and grooming.
  • A clinic with an emergency line in Valencia: the emergency number in large type and how to find the entrance at night.
  • A practice that offers home visits around Alicante: the towns it covers and how to request a visit.

Texts that name your town

Combine each service with your town, as in “dog grooming in Alicante”. Your vets and your reviews

For many owners, their pet is part of the family: before they trust you with it, they want to see who will treat it.

Vets with a name and a photo

Introduce your veterinary team with a photo, role and a note on each person's training and experience. If the clinic wishes, show each veterinarian's colegiado number, the membership number of the colegio de veterinarios; ask your colegio what it recommends.

Client reviews, as their authors wrote them

Invite owners to share their experience on Google, then show your rating and real reviews exactly as they were written, and reply to all of them, negative ones included. Don't invent, reword or select reviews to promise a result; see our guides to getting more Google reviews and responding to negative reviews.

Legal texts and data protection

A clinic's website has to show legal texts, and with WebAutonomos they are included:

  • Legal notice (aviso legal): who runs the website and how to reach you;
  • Privacy policy: what data you collect, why and for how long;
  • Cookie policy: which cookies the site uses and how to accept or reject them.

Keep your form short and don't ask for the animal's medical history. An important point: these texts don't replace the obligations of your profession, such as membership of your colegio or the authorisations and registrations your autonomous community requires. Check them with your colegio and your regional administration.

Common veterinary website mistakes

These mistakes, common on veterinary websites, drive away people who were about to call:

  1. Hiding the phone number and emergency hours.
  2. Leaving your Google profile out of date.
  3. Not showing your team or asking for reviews.
  4. A cluttered site that is slow or confusing on a phone.
  5. Promising results or publishing promotional prices.

Care with health messages

Animal health is a sensitive subject: avoid promises of recovery, promotional prices for medicines or treatments and testimonials other than your genuine reviews, and leave the diagnosis to the veterinarian.
