You have a website, but is it bringing you clients? To measure website performance, you need to know how many people visit, how they reach you, what they do, how many get in touch and how fast your website loads. This guide covers the metrics to follow in Google Analytics 4 (GA4) and other free tools, and how to review them in 10 minutes a week.

What is website performance?

Website performance is how well your site does its job: attracting the right visitors, giving them a good experience and getting some of them to call or write to you. The website performance metrics you follow should cover its two sides.

Commercial results: visitors, engagement and contacts

This side tells you whether the site actually brings in work: how many users arrive, through which channels, what content they read and how many call you, send a WhatsApp message or fill in your form.

Technical performance: speed and user experience

This side covers how fast each page loads and reacts to a tap. A slow website can lose potential clients before they read a word, especially on mobile.

Why measure website performance?

Without data, every decision about your website is a guess. Measuring tells you what works, so you make better decisions about your time and your marketing budget. It helps you:

  • See whether people searching for your services reach your site.
  • Spot problems early, such as a form that no longer sends messages or pages that have become slow.
  • Compare the performance of different marketing channels and campaigns.
  • Confirm whether a change, such as a clearer contact button, brings more enquiries or sales.

Choose your goals before your metrics

Before looking at any figures, decide what your website is for. A plumber in Alicante wants calls, a shoe shop in Elda wants online sales and a psychologist in Valencia wants appointment requests. Your goals decide which information deserves attention.

Turn each goal into a metric

Write down one or two goals and the metric that measures each one:

  • More quote requests: the number of form submissions.
  • More calls: taps on your phone number.
  • More WhatsApp messages: clicks on your WhatsApp button.
  • Being found for electrician in Elda: clicks and impressions for that query.

Which tools do you need?

Four tools cover the essentials for a small company, and a Google account is enough to use them:

  • GA4: visits, behaviour and the actions you mark as important.
  • Search Console: the queries where you appear and the clicks you get.
  • PageSpeed Insights: page speed and Core Web Vitals.
  • Google Business Profile: calls and direction requests from your listing.

Microsoft Clarity, also free, adds heatmaps and session recordings that show the experience of real visitors on a specific page.

Which website performance metrics should you track?

A handful of metrics is enough. Reviewed once a week, the website performance metrics in this table help you see whether your site attracts visitors, holds their attention and turns them into clients:

Metric Where to find it What it tells you
Users and sessions GA4, Acquisition How many people visit and how often they return
Traffic sources GA4, Traffic acquisition Which channel brings each visit
Engagement rate and time GA4, Acquisition and Engagement Whether your content holds attention
Contacts (key events) GA4, Engagement How many visits turn into calls, messages or forms
Clicks and impressions Search Console, Performance The searches you appear in and how many lead to a visit
Core Web Vitals PageSpeed Whether your website is fast and responsive
Calls and directions Business Profile, Performance Contacts that never reach your website

How many users visit your website?

GA4 answers this with three figures: users are the people who visit, sessions are their visits and views are the pages they open.

Users and sessions

In GA4 reports, the user figure is active users: distinct people who interact with your site, recognised by browser or device. One person on a phone and a laptop therefore usually counts twice, and someone who visits on Monday and Thursday adds one user and two sessions.

Landing pages and top pages

In the Engagement section, the Pages and screens report ranks your pages by views, and Landing page lists where visits start. If almost every visit lands on your home page and few reach your services, your menu may need a clearer path.

Compare with your own previous months

There is no universal good number of visits: performance depends on your trade, your area and the season. It's best to compare each month with the previous one and with the same month of the previous year.

GA4's demographic details also show which towns your users are in, so you can see whether visits come from your target area.

Traffic sources: which channels bring visits

GA4's Traffic acquisition section shows the channel behind each session, so you can see which parts of your marketing strategy bring visitors:

  • Organic Search: clicks on unpaid results.
  • Direct: typed addresses, bookmarks and visits without source information, such as links opened in some messaging apps.
  • Organic Social: your social media profiles and posts.
  • Referral: other sites that link to yours, such as an online directory.
  • Paid Search and Paid Social: your advertising campaigns.
  • Email: links in your newsletters, when they are tagged.

Each channel brings visits of different quality, so compare their contacts, not just their size. If your customers are local, organic traffic is worth growing over time; our local SEO guide explains how to improve it.

Tag your links to track your marketing

UTM tracking parameters (utm_source, utm_medium and utm_campaign) label the links you share in newsletters, WhatsApp statuses or a flyer's QR code, so GA4 knows where each visit came from and which campaigns perform better. Engagement metrics: time spent on your website

GA4 measures engagement with engaged sessions: those that last longer than 10 seconds (the default setting), include a key event or have more than one page view.

Engagement rate and bounce rate

Engagement rate is the share of engaged sessions; bounce rate is the opposite, the share of sessions that were not engaged. Someone who reads your content for a while and leaves without clicking is therefore not a bounce in GA4.

Average engagement time per active user measures how long your site stayed in the foreground of the screen.

What a high bounce rate can mean

When a page has many sessions without engagement, the cause is often one of these issues:

  • It is slow to load, especially on mobile.
  • The content doesn't match what the visitor searched for.
  • The design makes a bad first impression, or the phone number is hard to spot.
  • Something is broken, such as a form or a button.

Bounce rates vary between pages, so compare each page with itself over time. And a short visit isn't always bad: if someone sees your number and calls, your website has done its job.

Conversion metrics: calls, forms and WhatsApp messages

This is the metric that matters most: how many visitors actually get in touch. A conversion, which GA4 now calls a ‘key event’, is any important action that turns a visitor into a potential client:

  • Taps on your phone number.
  • Clicks on your WhatsApp button.
  • Contact or quote form submissions.
  • Clicks on the link to directions or to the booking tool you already use.

Set up contact tracking

Enhanced measurement records some actions on its own, such as clicks on links that leave your site (the WhatsApp button, for instance). Others, like phone taps or form submissions, may need extra setup. Once an action appears in your list of events, mark it as a key event.

Conversion rate

Your conversion rate is the share of sessions that end in a contact. Conversion rates vary between sectors, so follow yours month by month. If you're getting visits but few contacts, something is holding people back, such as a hidden WhatsApp button, a long form or unclear content; our checklist of the elements every professional website needs can help.

Contacts that bypass your website

Some clients call you directly from your Google listing. If your profile is verified, its Performance section shows those calls, direction requests and website clicks, and our tips to optimise your listing can help you improve them. For word of mouth, the simplest way is to ask each new customer how they found you.

Queries and clicks in Search Console

Search Console is the basic tool for SEO: it tells you which queries display your pages and how many people click. Its Performance section gives four figures:

  • Clicks: how many people clicked through to your site.
  • Impressions: the number of times you appeared, even without a click.
  • Average CTR: the click-through rate, or share of impressions that led to a click.
  • Average position: your average ranking, with 1 as the top spot. It can move without anything important changing.

Don't expect these clicks to match the visits in GA4 exactly: the two tools count different things.

Use your queries to plan new content

The Queries tab lists the searches that bring impressions, although some rare queries are hidden to protect privacy. If you are a plumber in Alicante with many impressions but few clicks for emergency plumber Alicante, a dedicated page or a better title can help.

These queries are a source of ideas for your content strategy, and our article on local keyword research explains how to choose the right keywords.

Make sure your pages are indexed

The Pages section lists which URLs are indexed and why others were left out. Open it after publishing anything new, because a page that isn't indexed can't appear in the results.

Speed and user experience: measuring technical performance

To measure the technical performance of a page, enter its address in PageSpeed Insights. You get its Core Web Vitals, a performance score and suggestions for improvement.

Core Web Vitals in plain words

These three metrics describe the user experience of a page:

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): how long the biggest block of content, often an image or a headline, takes to appear.
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP): how quickly the page reacts to a tap, click or keystroke. It replaced First Input Delay (FID).
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): whether text and buttons jump around during loading.

Each one is rated good, needs improvement or poor; aim for good on all three.

Real visits and simulated tests

When a page gets enough visits, PageSpeed displays data from real visitors using the Chrome browser over the previous weeks; otherwise, you only get a simulated test, still useful for spotting issues.

Results differ between mobile and desktop, and mobile is the most important, since many people will look you up on their phone.

Common causes of slow pages

  • Heavy images that take long to load because they haven't been resized or compressed.
  • Too many plugins, scripts or embedded widgets.
  • A slow server: the Time to First Byte (TTFB) in PageSpeed reflects the server response time.
  • Fonts, videos or pop-ups that load before your content.

After testing, fix your most visited pages first: that is where better performance pays off. Our article on why website speed affects your ranking goes into more detail.

How to set up your measurement tools

You don't need to be technical. These steps summarise the official help centre:

  1. Open GA4 with your Google account, then create an account and a property with your time zone and currency.
  2. Add a web data stream with your website address.
  3. Paste the measurement ID (it starts with G-) or the tag snippet into your site; many website builders have a field for it.
  4. Visit your site and open the Realtime report: your visit appears within moments, while standard reports take a little longer.
  5. Define your own connection as internal traffic and activate the filter, so your own visits don't inflate the figures.
  6. Mark the contacts you want to follow as key events (GA4's name for conversions).
  7. Add your site to Search Console, verify it (with the GA4 tag, an HTML file or a DNS record) and link the two tools.

If you'd rather not touch the code, ask whoever manages your website to add the tag: it only needs doing once.

Cookies and consent in Spain

GA4 uses cookies. In Spain, your website must ask visitors for consent before setting analytics cookies, usually through a banner where they can accept or reject them, and your cookie policy has to mention them.

Visitors who reject cookies may be missing from GA4, which can then display fewer users than really visit; Search Console clicks don't depend on your cookies.

If WebAutonomos builds your website, the legal texts (legal notice, privacy policy and cookie policy) are included; when you add a tool such as GA4, make sure the cookie policy mentions it.

How often should you analyse your website performance?

Once a week works best for a quick look at your metrics, with more time once a month. Small daily changes don't matter: one day's figures move with a bank holiday, the weather or a single social media post.

A 10-minute weekly check

  • Users and sessions compared with the previous week.
  • Contacts: calls, WhatsApp messages and forms.
  • Traffic sources, to spot any sudden change.

A monthly review

  • The queries that gained or lost clicks in Search Console.
  • Calls and direction requests from your profile.
  • A PageSpeed test of your most visited pages.
  • A comparison with the same month of the previous year.

Realistic goals for a freelancer

Measure each goal in contacts, not visits: a plumber or a neighbourhood shop gains more from an extra call each month than from visitors in other provinces who will never hire them.

For example, aim for more contacts than in the same month of the previous year, or more clicks from searches such as physiotherapist in Elche. Note the date of every change you make, including marketing campaigns, so you can see its effect later.

Measuring website performance isn't complicated if you stick to a few performance metrics, spend 10 minutes a week on them and keep one question in mind: are more people contacting you than before? The answer tells you what to improve next.

Frequently asked questions

How do I measure website performance as a small business?
Use four free tools: GA4 for users, traffic sources and contacts, Search Console for searches, PageSpeed Insights for speed and Core Web Vitals, and your Business Profile for calls. Spend 10 minutes a week on them and compare each month with the previous ones.
Do I need technical knowledge to use Google Analytics?
No. You create an account, add your site and paste a short code, and the basic reports become clear with practice. Whoever manages your website can do the setup for you.
Is Google Analytics free?
Yes. The standard version is free and you only need a Google account to use it.
How many visits should my website have?
It depends on your sector and area: a local electrician can get work from a modest number of visits if they come from nearby people looking for that service. Compare each month with your own previous months and aim for more contacts.
How often should I review the metrics?
A quick weekly check of users, traffic sources and contacts is enough, for example 10 minutes every Monday, plus a longer look once a month.
What do I do if my conversion rate is very low?
Make sure the WhatsApp button is easy to see on mobile, that your form asks only for the essentials and that your main message is clear as soon as your website opens. Then confirm in GA4 that those clicks and form submissions are recorded.
