Many people look up a fitness coach online before they book a first session, and you have to show them, in seconds, who you help, how you work and how to start. This guide covers the sections and content your website needs, website design tips, prices and mistakes, with examples for personal trainers in Valencia, Alicante or Elche.

It's written for fitness coaches who coach in person, at home or by video call. If you're a life coach or a career coach, our article for coaches is a closer fit.

Why do personal trainers need a website?

Personal trainers need a website because it is the one place they control: it presents their services, pricing and experience, appears in search results and answers questions while they focus on their sessions. Many fitness businesses use Instagram to attract followers and their site to convert them into clients.

A social profile shows your workouts, but the platform decides who sees them, and it often leaves out what a new client needs to know before getting in touch: exactly where you train, how your sessions run and what it costs. Your own corner of the internet puts those answers in one place.

Things people usually want to know

  • who you work with, such as beginners or runners;
  • which service fits them: personal training, online coaching or group fitness classes;
  • where you train: a gym, a park or at home;
  • your pricing and how to get started.

First decisions: audience, offer and location

Everything begins before the first colour is chosen. Three decisions shape what follows:

  • your audience: the kind of client you actually want to train, such as beginners, runners preparing for a race, older adults working on strength or busy parents;
  • your main offer: one-to-one personal training, small group classes or coaching by video call, and the single action you want each new client to take, such as sending a WhatsApp message or asking for a first assessment;
  • your location: the town and neighbourhoods you cover, and whether you travel to clients' homes.

Speaking to one group helps your ideal visitor recognise their own needs in your texts, and the single action becomes the button you repeat from top to bottom. Together, these answers are the starting point for your headline, what you offer and the searches you want to appear in. Try to sum up each one in a sentence: if you can't, a reader won't understand it quickly either.

What should a personal trainer website include?

You need a homepage, an about page, your services and pricing, a contact page and the legal texts. You don't need dozens of pages: a clear structure makes everything easy to find in one or two clicks.

Homepage

Be specific: say clearly who you help, where you train and how to start, with a strong headline and one clear button that helps convert visitors. The flow then continues with your services and a few lines about you, so that each person sees quickly whether you suit them.

About page

Describe your approach: why you started coaching, the kind of person you coach and what a first month with you involves. List only the qualifications you can prove, for example a degree in sports science (Ciencias de la Actividad Física y del Deporte), a vocational qualification (ciclo formativo) or one obtained in the UK: rules on sports professions vary between regions. See building a memorable brand.

Services and pricing

Give each service its own section and a name, as you would a product: one-to-one personal training, online coaching, group classes or monthly plans, with what each includes, how you adapt it to each person's needs and your class times. Clear pricing avoids back-and-forth, builds trust and filters out enquiries that don't fit; if you'd rather not publish it, explain how to ask. When your rates vary, a range for each option already helps a new client understand whether you're within reach.

In person, at home or by video call

Many coaches combine several formats. Give each one its own section, so that every visitor goes straight to what they're looking for:

  • at a gym or studio: the address, parking and the equipment you use;
  • at home: the towns or neighbourhoods you cover and any travel charge, because in Valencia or Alicante the first question is often whether you come to them;
  • remote coaching: how it works week by week, how often you update the plan and which app or platform you use;
  • group classes: levels, days and the number of places.

A video-call programme is a product someone buys without meeting you, so its section is designed as a sales pitch: who it suits, what it covers, how long it lasts and how to join. Explain clearly, step by step, what happens after someone signs up: the first call, the plan and the weekly review. Clients can follow it from anywhere in Spain, and it's worth adding a video of you coaching for someone who has never seen you working.

Personal trainer website design: examples and tips

Good design for a PT website is about being clear, not effects. Browse a few round-ups of examples from other fitness coaches, and the ones that do the job share the same habits.

What great examples have in common

  • they say exactly who they help, and where, in the first line;
  • they ask for a single action and repeat the same button everywhere;
  • they show real pictures of the coach in action, not generic images;
  • they give proof anyone can verify: feedback shared with permission, public ratings and qualifications;
  • they publish real prices or a range, or explain clearly how to get them;
  • they make the next step obvious, with a WhatsApp button that takes seconds to use;
  • they load quickly and are designed for phones first.

A strong first screen

The first screen typically combines a photo, a headline and a button. For example: “Strength workouts for beginners in Elche, at the gym or by video call”, with “Book your first session”. Anyone should know in seconds whether you're the right coach.

Your own photos rather than stock images

Stock images appear on many other sites; pictures of you coaching or of your fitness studio show what a session actually looks like. Ask clients for written permission before they appear in photos. Colours, fonts and mobile

Two or three colours, readable fonts and white space give your fitness website a consistent look. Make sure it's built for mobile, as many potential clients will look you up on their phone, and that pages load fast (see website speed and search rankings).

Plain, specific content

Write as you would talk to a new client face to face: brief sentences, plain words and practical details, such as how long a workout lasts, what to bring and where to park. Content written like this helps a reader understand your approach. Plain words also help you get found, because search engines rely on the same vocabulary as the person typing into the search bar. If writing isn't your thing, our tips for writing your own texts can help.

From first visit to first conversation

Every section should point to the same next step: a WhatsApp message, a call or a quick note. A single button that is easy to find guides a visitor better than a dozen links, and a prompt reply does the rest. Think of the path a visitor takes: they land on a section, scan it in a few seconds, then decide whether to message you.

Ask only for the essentials

Show your phone number, a WhatsApp button and a short booking form that asks only for a name, an email address or phone number, and the kind of coaching they're looking for. It's better to keep anything about health for your first meeting: health data is sensitive. Each additional field gives someone a reason to stop before they send their enquiry. It's worth sending yourself a test enquiry before you go live.

A link to the scheduling app you already use

If you already take bookings through an app that shows your open slots, a button that links to it lets anyone choose a time themselves; if the same app handles payments, one link covers both. With WebAutonomos, we add it as a plain link to your tool, and enquiries also reach your inbox and your WhatsApp. Still choosing one? See how to choose a scheduling tool.

Reply quickly

Answer enquiries as soon as you can, even with a few words to arrange a first chat and ask about their needs. Ready-made answers for the requests you receive every day, such as prices, availability or where you train, make replying faster. A prompt, friendly reply is a great way to show the visitor there is a real person behind the screen, and it builds trust before they start working with you.

Agencies abroad or a team that knows Spain?

Several of the agencies that cater for fitness coaches charge in pounds. They may suit you, but if you live and coach in Spain, it's worth checking three things before you hire a company abroad:

  • that the legal notice and the privacy policy follow Spanish rules (the LSSI and the GDPR);
  • that the texts target the searches made here, in English and in Spanish;
  • who makes changes later on, and how quickly.

Ask to see real examples they have made, and find out who will answer your messages.

Marketing and SEO: getting found near you

Searches in your town

For SEO, put your town and services in your page titles and texts, using the searches people actually type: “best personal trainer in Valencia”, “trainer near me” or “English-speaking PT in Alicante”. Use the same name, address and phone number everywhere, including your Google Business Profile listing (step-by-step article). Someone looking for a coach in their area often sees the map and the listings shown above the search results before they click through to you, so complete your profile with your opening hours, pictures and prices: that may be where someone will find you first.

Newsletters and subscribers

Put your domain name in your Instagram bio; a free download, such as a beginner's workout plan, can act as a lead magnet and gather subscribers (see our email marketing guide).

Trust, promises and legal texts

Testimonials with permission

Real testimonials and reviews are the proof that helps you build trust. Publish them only with each client's written permission, link to your reviews on Maps (see how to get more Google reviews) and, in any case, never invent or edit one. Social proof is more convincing when anyone can trace it back to a real name rather than anonymous praise.

No promises of results

Describe what you do, not what each client will achieve: no promises of weight loss, health improvements or a body transformation, and no diet plans unless you're qualified.

Legal texts

Because you collect names and telephone numbers, you need a privacy policy (GDPR), a legal notice (aviso legal) required by Spain's LSSI and, if you use analytics, a cookie policy (see legal notices, privacy and cookies explained). With WebAutonomos, these legal texts are part of the package.

Common website mistakes personal trainers make

  • relying only on your social profiles;
  • no pricing and no clear way to book;
  • speaking to everyone instead of a specific audience;
  • not saying exactly where you train;
  • a different button in every section instead of one call to action;
  • generic images instead of pictures of you;
  • promising results instead of showing proof;
  • a fitness website that is hard to use on a phone.
