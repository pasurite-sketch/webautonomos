Look up “plumber in Valencia” on your mobile and you'll often see a map with a few nearby businesses above the organic search results. That block is the Map Pack, and being in it can bring a freelancer or small business in Spain more visibility, calls and visits. Here is how to rank in the Google Map Pack, step by step, based on Google's own help pages and policies rather than third-party studies.

What is the Google Map Pack?

The Google Map Pack, also called the Local Pack, is the block of results with a map and a few nearby businesses that appear for local searches. Each entry comes from a Google Business Profile, formerly Google My Business.

Each business in it shows details such as its name, its stars, whether it's open and buttons to call or get directions; tapping one opens its full listing. To learn how to show up on the map itself, see how to appear on Google Maps.

Which searches trigger it?

It appears when someone is looking for a place or a business nearby: “dentist Alicante”, “electrician near me” or “English-speaking lawyer in Benidorm”. Informational searches such as “how to bleed a radiator” rarely trigger it, and the businesses shown change with the searcher's location.

How are Map Pack rankings decided?

Google's help article with tips to improve your local ranking says local search results are based mainly on three factors: relevance, distance and prominence. There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

  • Relevance: how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for. Detailed categories and a clear description help you match relevant searches such as “emergency plumber in Elche”.
  • Distance: how far each business is from the searcher's location or the place named in the query.
  • Prominence: how well known a business is, including how many sites link to it and how many reviews it has. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your ranking.

Distance is the factor you can do least about. The other two factors you can influence, though not control, through signals such as accurate information, consistent details, genuine reviews and a site that confirms what you do and where.

Paid ads are a separate route. Google Ads can show your business as a sponsored result in Search and on Google Maps, and Local Services Ads are also available in some countries and sectors. Ads stop when the budget does; see our beginner's guide to Google Ads.

Create, claim and verify your Google Business Profile

Start by checking that your business is eligible. Google's eligibility and ownership guidelines say it must deal with clients in person during its stated opening times, and whoever creates or claims its entry must be the owner or someone authorised to represent it. Online-only businesses and rental or for-sale properties, such as a holiday flat, aren't eligible.

Creating one is free with a Google account. If your business already appears on the map, find the existing entry and claim it rather than starting a duplicate; if someone else already controls it, you can request access from the current owner.

Then verify it. Google says verification tells it you're authorised to represent the business, so it's more likely to show up in search results. Depending on your business, you may be asked for a short video as proof. If verification stalls, get in touch with Google support rather than starting again from scratch. If you need more help, try the official forum: you can post to the Help Community, get answers from community members and replies from volunteer Product Experts.

Use your real business name

Write the name customers know, as on your sign and invoices. Google's guidelines forbid adding keywords, towns or slogans, and breaking them can lead to suspension: your trading name on its own is fine, the same name followed by “cheap plumber Valencia” is not.

Premises or service area

If customers visit you, enter your location exactly as it appears on your site, with the postcode.

If you travel to them, as many plumbers or mobile hairdressers do, Google asks you to hide the address and set a service area. Choose the towns you really cover, such as Elche, Santa Pola and Crevillent, rather than the whole province.

Who should own and manage your Business Profile?

You should: keep ownership on your own account and give anyone else, such as team members or the agency that handles your marketing, manager access instead. Several users can share access, and each user has a role:

  • Primary owner: there's only one, and they can transfer that role to another owner.
  • Owners: can manage everything, including adding or removing users and removing the business from Google.
  • Managers: can manage most day-to-day tasks, such as editing details and replying to feedback, but can't remove the business or manage users.

You'll find these roles in the settings of your profile, where invitations go out by email when you add a new user. When an employee leaves or you change agency, remove their access straight away to avoid problems in the future. This matters because getting control back from a former supplier who is the only owner can be slow, and the same is true of an account a relative set up years ago; in both cases, support can help you request access.

Fill in every section and keep it up to date

According to Google's guidance, businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to show up in local search results. You can edit your details directly from Search, by looking up your business name while signed in to the account that manages the profile, or in the Maps app; some changes are reviewed before they're published, and other users can suggest an edit to your details. Primary category and secondary categories

Your category says what kind of business you are. For the primary category, choose the option that best describes your main activity, as specific as possible; then add secondary categories only for related work you really do. Our guide on choosing your business categories has examples by trade.

Services, hours and description

  • Services and products: add what you offer in the words customers use, such as “boiler repair” or “gel nails”.
  • Hours: regular opening times plus special hours for public holidays, the Fallas or an August closure.
  • Phone and website: a number you answer and the most relevant page of your site.
  • Description: tell clients what your business does and where, without links or price offers; say so if you serve clients in English.
  • Attributes: such as wheelchair access or accepted payment methods.

NAP consistency and citations

NAP stands for name, address and phone number. Write them exactly the same everywhere:

  • your listing and your site's footer and contact page;
  • Facebook, Instagram and other social media platforms;
  • listings on directories such as Páginas Amarillas and on trade association sites.

Google gathers information about each business from several sources, including edits and pictures sent directly from users. If one says “Calle Mayor 12”, another “C/ Mayor, 12, bajo” and an old listing shows a previous number, it's harder to know which details are right. Consistent details send a clear signal to Google, build trust and stop customers calling a number that no longer works.

A citation is any mention of your business with its NAP on another site, such as directories and business listings. Links from a club you sponsor, a fair you participate in or a regional newspaper are also signals of prominence. You'll learn how in our guides on local citations and earning links in your town.

Reviews: ask honestly and reply to every one

Opinions from clients are one of the prominence signals. Asking for feedback directly after each job also shows you what to improve, and replying shows customers, present and future, that you value what they say.

How to ask for a review

  1. From your Business Profile, create a short link or a QR code that takes clients straight to the right place, allowing them to leave their opinion in seconds; print it for the counter or your invoices.
  2. Ask every client once the job is done, not only those you think are happy.
  3. Share it the same day by WhatsApp or email and let them write in their own words.

Google's policy on content posted by users forbids, among other things:

  • incentives such as discounts, gifts or free extras in exchange for a review;
  • content that doesn't reflect a genuine experience, such as fake or bought opinions;
  • opinions about your own business posted by you or your staff, or about a competitor;
  • discouraging negative reviews or asking only satisfied customers.

Content that breaks these rules can be removed, so never buy opinions or offer anything in return for them. To learn more, see how to get more Google reviews.

Respond to reviews, including negative ones

Thank each customer briefly; replies are public and build trust, and you manage them from your account. With a negative one, stay calm, acknowledge the problem, give the facts without arguing and offer to sort it out privately. If a comment breaks Google's policies, for instance spam or something off-topic, you can report it; a fair but critical one stays up. See how to reply to negative Google reviews.

Photos and updates that show an active business

Google's tips include adding photos and videos that show what you offer. Real, good-quality pictures build trust; skip stock images and heavy filters, and show:

  • your shopfront as seen from the street;
  • the team and your van;
  • finished jobs, products or dishes.

Updates about news, offers or events are a different matter: they aren't among Google's ranking tips, but they show customers that the business is active. Post at a regular rhythm you can sustain; see how to publish Google Posts and photos and videos on your Google listing.

What role does your site play?

A site isn't required to have a Business Profile, but it helps: a company's official site is one of Google's sources, and links to it count towards prominence. Your site also competes for the organic listings, so organic SEO and local SEO belong in the same strategy.

Pages for each service and town

Give each of your main services its own page, with the service and town in the title, such as “Boiler repair in Alicante”; a little keyword research shows which words clients actually type. Create a page for another town only where you really work and have useful content to offer.

Consistent details and LocalBusiness markup

Show the same contact details and opening times as your Business Profile. LocalBusiness structured data, a short block of JSON-LD code, describes those details in a machine-readable format; it can help Google understand your business but won't put it in the Map Pack on its own. Google's developer documentation and related resources set out the properties to use, such as the name, postal details, telephone and opening times, and its Rich Results Test lets you check your markup. How to measure your progress

Each month, open the Performance section of your profile to learn:

  • views on Google Search and Google Maps, which show your visibility;
  • the searches that found your business;
  • calls, direction requests and website clicks.

Also try searching for your service and town from your mobile, in different neighbourhoods: you'll find that the businesses shown depend on location. Daily changes in rankings are normal; over time, the trend shows whether your local search visibility is growing. For your site, see these basic metrics.

Common mistakes that hold back your rankings

Avoid these common mistakes:

  • keywords or town names in the business name;
  • duplicate profiles, or an old one nobody manages;
  • an agency or a former employee as the only owner;
  • a mailbox or premises where you don't receive clients;
  • an old phone number on your site or in online listings;
  • paid-for or rewarded ratings, and complaints left unanswered;
  • opening times you forget to edit for holidays;
  • town pages with copied content.

Be wary, too, of anyone who promises a top-three place by a fixed date: these positions aren't for sale.

A plan for the next few months

Getting into the Map Pack takes time, and there's no official timeline: it depends on your competitors, your town and your trade. Taking these steps in order keeps things manageable:

  1. Month 1: check eligibility, create or claim your listing, verify and complete it, and add real pictures.
  2. Month 2: ask every client for an opinion, reply to what they write and make your details identical everywhere.
  3. Month 3: give each service its own section on your site and look for a few links from nearby organisations.
  4. Month 4 onwards: check your Performance figures, post regular updates and keep your details in good shape.

Frequently asked questions

Can I pay to appear in the Map Pack?
No. Google states that there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Google Ads are a separate, paid route: the ads are labelled as sponsored and stop when you stop paying.
How long does it take to rank in the Map Pack?
There's no official timeline. It depends on competition in your town and trade, how detailed your Google Business Profile is and the reviews you receive. Treat it as a medium-term strategy and check your profile's figures every month.
Do I need a website for the Map Pack?
No, but it helps. Google also uses information from a company's official site, and links from other sites show how well known your business is. Show the same details on your site as on your Business Profile.
Why do some people see my business in the Map Pack and others don't?
Distance is one of Google's three local ranking factors, so results change with the searcher's location. Check from several neighbourhoods and nearby towns, not only from your premises.
Can I get into the Map Pack without premises?
Yes. If you work at customers' homes, you can have a Business Profile as a service-area business: Google asks you to hide your address and add the areas you serve. Keep them to towns you really cover.
