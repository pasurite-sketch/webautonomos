Email marketing for small business means sending planned, useful emails to people who know you or have asked to hear from you. It's a channel you own, and you can start without spending a euro. Whether you're a plumber in Valencia, an electrician in Alicante or a hairdresser in Elche, this guide explains how to choose a platform, build your email list legally, prepare campaigns and automation, write better emails and measure the results.

Is email marketing worth it for a small business?

Yes. Email marketing is cheap, personal and easy to start: you write to people who already know you and your work, on a channel that doesn't depend on an algorithm. For freelancers and small businesses that live on repeat business and word of mouth, it can help you stay in touch with clients between jobs.

You don't need a marketing team or a big audience, just a realistic strategy. A useful email can remind a customer that a service is due, ask for a review or share a seasonal promotion: it keeps building your brand and can increase repeat sales without paying for ads.

Your email list belongs to you

On social media, the platform decides who sees your posts. Your list is yours: if you change software, you can export it and take it with you. Social media helps new clients discover you (see which social networks suit the self-employed); email keeps the conversation going with those who have already chosen you.

How to choose an email marketing platform

Look for platforms that let you begin at no cost, with an easy editor and the basic legal features built in. You can switch later.

Free plans to get started

You don't need to spend a single euro to get started. Brevo (formerly Sendinblue) lets you store up to 100,000 contacts and send up to 300 emails per day for free. Mailchimp offers up to 250 contacts and 500 sends per month. MailerLite allows up to 250 subscribers and 2,500 emails per month (limits as of September 2026). If you expect your list to grow, Brevo's free plan leaves more room for growth, although its daily cap can spread larger sends over several days; for a small list, any of the three can meet your needs. These options can change, so compare the current terms before you decide.

Features to look for

  • Sign-up forms to embed on your site or share on social media.
  • Consent tools: an unticked box and, ideally, double opt-in.
  • Automatic unsubscribe links in every email.
  • Templates and a drag-and-drop editor to help you create emails without coding.
  • Reports on opens, clicks and unsubscribes.
  • Segmentation and automation, even if you only use them later.

Find out whether automation or segmentation is reserved for paid plans. At the beginning, free email marketing software may be enough; once your list is growing and harder to manage, compare what an upgrade adds.

How to build your email list from scratch

Building a list is the foundation of email marketing, and there are two ways to grow it: existing customers who agree to join and visitors who sign up on your site. The important thing is that every subscriber wants your emails.

Starting with the customers you already have

Your invoices, quotes and WhatsApp chats hold plenty of addresses, but having someone's email doesn't automatically mean you can add them to your list. Ask clearly when you finish a job: ‘Would you like to receive my monthly tips by email?’

Add a sign-up form to your website

A clear form turns a visitor into a subscriber. Ask only for a name and an email address, add an unticked consent box, explain what subscribers will receive and how often, and point to your privacy policy. Keep it separate from your contact form: someone asking for a quote hasn't agreed to join your list. Offer something useful in return, such as a seasonal checklist; you can also create a landing page whose only goal is the sign-up. Our guide to the elements every freelancer's site must have helps you decide where the form fits.

What does Spanish law say about email marketing?

In general, you need the recipient's prior, express consent before you send commercial emails, with one exception for existing customers, and you must handle their personal data transparently. An adviser, such as a gestor or a lawyer, can help with your own situation.

Consent under the LSSI, and the exception for existing customers

Article 21 of the LSSI, Spain's law on information society services and e-commerce, prohibits promotional emails that the recipient hasn't requested or expressly authorised beforehand. The exception covers anyone you already have a contractual relationship with: if you obtained a customer's address lawfully when they hired you or bought from you, you can email them about your own products or services similar to the ones they bought. In return, you must offer an easy, free option to refuse these emails, both when you collect the address and in every email you send. If in doubt, ask for consent. Every commercial email must also make clear that it's a commercial communication, identify the sender and include a working unsubscribe link.

GDPR: transparency with subscribers

An email address is personal data. Under the GDPR, you must tell subscribers who you are, why you collect their address, how long you'll keep it and what their rights are, in the policy linked next to your form. It's also important to record each consent with its date and source. The Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (AEPD), Spain's data protection authority, provides Facilita RGPD, a free tool for businesses whose processing is low-risk: a helpful starting point.

Segmentation: the right message for each group

Segmentation means dividing your list into groups so that each person receives what is relevant to them: a regular client and someone who only asked for a quote need different emails. Useful segments for small businesses in Spain include new and regular clients, town or neighbourhood, the service they bought and enquiries that haven't turned into a job yet. If your audience mixes expats and locals on the Costa Blanca, segmenting by language can make a real difference. Over time, segmentation can be based on behaviour, such as a follow-up only for those who clicked on a promotion, making each email more relevant.

Types of email campaigns for small businesses

Choose your campaigns according to your goals: being remembered, filling your diary, winning back past clients or collecting reviews. A few types that suit your business best, repeated regularly, make a solid email marketing strategy, and your readers will know what to expect.

Welcome email

The first email a new subscriber receives arrives while their interest is fresh: a great moment to introduce yourself and your brand, say what you'll send and how often, and deliver what you promised, building trust from the start.

Monthly newsletter

A regular email with a useful tip, an update on your business and, now and then, a special deal. One main idea per email is easier to read, and templates make each issue quicker to create. If you have a blog, the newsletter is a good way to share your latest content.

Offers and seasonal reminders

If the services you provide repeat, a reminder at the right time can help both of you: the annual boiler service, the next appointment at the salon. A promotion in a quiet month can also increase bookings: say what you're offering, until when and under what conditions, and alternate offers with helpful content so your emails aren't all about sales.

Review and referral requests

After a job, thank the customer and ask for a Google review with a direct link to your profile (how to get more Google reviews has tips on asking). Clients who had a good experience can also recommend you to friends, a great way to win new customers through someone they already trust.

Marketing automation for beginners: three simple sequences

Automation means setting up emails once so your platform sends them on its own when something happens, such as a new subscription or a finished job. Three sequences cover the essentials and save time every week.

Welcome sequence

When someone joins your list, the welcome email goes out straight away. A second email a few days later can include your most useful tips, and a third can explain how to book or ask for a quote.

After a job or a purchase

A few days after a job, an automatic email thanks the customer, asks for a review and reminds them how to reach you. If the service repeats, schedule a reminder for when it's next due, or suggest a related service. Many tools trigger these emails when you add a customer to a particular list or group.

Follow-up after a quote

When someone asks for a quote and doesn't reply, a brief, personal follow-up a week later can answer their doubts, making it easier to say yes; stop after one or two reminders. Review each automation now and then; to decide what else to automate, see which tasks to automate first and no-code automation for small business.

How to write a marketing email that works

A good email comes down to three things: a strong first impression in the inbox, useful content and one clear call to action.

Subject line and sender name

Keep the subject line short, specific and honest: ‘Time for your boiler service’ tells readers more than ‘News from us’. Leave out capitals and exaggerated promises, which can trigger spam filters. Use your own name or your business name as the sender, and if your tool allows it, test two versions to learn which works best.

Content and design

Write as you'd talk to a customer: one topic, brief paragraphs that are quick to read and practical value, such as a tip based on your experience. Keep the design clean and in your brand colours, because many readers will see your email on their phone. If you're short of ideas, the tips in how to write content when you're not a writer apply to emails too.

One clear call to action

End each email with a single next step: call you, message you on WhatsApp, book a visit or leave a review. Point readers to a page that matches the email, so the conversion happens without extra searching.

How to keep your emails out of the spam folder

Deliverability is whether your emails land in the inbox rather than the spam folder. A few habits make the difference:

  • Authenticate your domain: your email marketing platform explains how to add SPF, DKIM and DMARC records, which show mailbox providers that your emails really come from you.
  • Use your own domain: write from an address such as info@yourbusiness.es; many providers advise against sending from a Gmail or Hotmail address.
  • Keep your list clean: remove addresses that bounce and, now and then, subscribers who haven't opened or clicked anything for a long while.
  • Keep a regular rhythm: a sudden burst after months of silence can look suspicious.
  • Make unsubscribing easy: someone who can't find the option may mark you as spam instead.

How to measure your email marketing results

After each campaign, review four indicators: opens, clicks and unsubscribes, which email marketing tools report for you, and conversions, which you note yourself. There's no universal benchmark across businesses, so compare each campaign with your previous ones and learn from the differences.

Indicator What it tells you What to check if it gets worse
Open rate How many recipients opened your email. The sender name, the subject and the timing.
Click rate How many recipients clicked through to your site. The body of the email, and how clear the button is.
Unsubscribes How many asked to stop receiving your emails. Your rhythm, and whether your emails match what the form promised.
Conversions Calls, WhatsApp messages, quote requests or sales after the email. Whether the link leads to an obvious next step.

Treat open rates with caution: some email apps, such as Apple Mail, can load emails automatically, so an email may count as opened even if nobody read it. Click rates, replies and enquiries give more reliable insights. As for timing, no single day or hour suits every audience: try different days over several campaigns and keep what works for your business. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying a list of addresses: nobody on it has asked to hear from you, so emailing them breaches the LSSI, and your messages can end up in spam.
  • Taking consent for granted with a pre-ticked box, or adding everyone who fills in your enquiry form.
  • Putting all recipients in the visible To or CC field, which shows every customer's address to everyone else.
  • Writing only sales emails, with nothing useful in between.
  • Disappearing for months, then sending several emails in a row.
  • Emailing everyone the same message when a segment would make it more relevant.
  • Never looking at the results, so you keep making the same mistakes.

Getting started: your first month

Starting from scratch, you can be up and running in a few weeks:

  1. Choose the tool that suits you best and set your sender details.
  2. Prepare the sign-up form and its consent wording, then add it to your site.
  3. Ask your existing customers whether they'd like to join, and import only those who say yes.
  4. Write your welcome email and switch on the welcome automation.
  5. Plan your first campaigns: a useful monthly email, plus reminders when services are due.
  6. After each campaign, check opens, clicks and unsubscribes, and adjust the next one.

Frequently asked questions

Does email marketing work for small businesses and freelancers?
Yes, and you don't need a large list. A freelancer knows their clients' needs and can write in a personal tone, for example with a reminder about the annual boiler service. What matters is that every recipient asked to hear from you.
Is there free email marketing software for small businesses?
Yes. Several platforms, including Brevo, Mailchimp and MailerLite, offer a free plan with limits on how many addresses you can store and how many emails you can send. These limits change, so compare the current terms and make sure the plan includes a sign-up form, an automatic unsubscribe option and basic reports.
Do I need consent to email my customers in Spain?
In general, yes. Spain's LSSI requires the recipient's prior, express consent for commercial emails, and the GDPR applies to how you handle their data. The exception is existing customers: you can email them about your own products or services similar to the ones they bought, provided you obtained their address lawfully and offered an easy, free option to refuse when you collected it and in every email. If in doubt, ask for consent and check your case with an adviser.
What is double opt-in?
Double opt-in is an extra step in which a new subscriber must click a link in an email to confirm their address. It stops anyone from signing up someone else's address and gives you proof of consent. Many email marketing tools let you turn it on, and it's best practice even with a modest list.
How often should you send marketing emails?
Once a month is a good rhythm to begin with: often enough that clients don't forget you, not so often that they tire of you. Add an extra email when you have news or a seasonal offer. Regularity matters more than volume: one email a month all year is better than a burst followed by months of silence.
How do I know if my email marketing is working?
There's no universal open rate to aim for: it depends on your sector, your list and how well your audience knows you. Compare each campaign with your previous ones, and watch clicks, replies and enquiries as well as opens. If they drop, review how your emails appear in the inbox and how often you write.
