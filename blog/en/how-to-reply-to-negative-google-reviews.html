How to respond to negative reviews on Google without losing customers

You open your business listing and there it is: a bad review, one star, written in anger. Sooner or later, many freelancers and small firms in Valencia, Alicante, Elda and Elche go through this. Take a breath: the way you respond can show every potential client how you work.

In short: wait until you're calm, thank the reviewer, acknowledge the problem, offer a solution and take the conversation offline. Below you'll find each step, example responses you can adapt and what to do about fake reviews.

Why respond to negative reviews?

Negative reviews are public, and so is your reply: the person who complained will read it, and so will everyone comparing you with your competitors. Treat each complaint as a customer service request. You can't control what people write, but you can control how you respond.

What you gain by replying

Google's help pages present feedback as an opportunity to learn what people expect and find ways to improve. A thoughtful response:

  • shows that you take criticism seriously and care about your customers;
  • lets the reviewer know you've listened: they're notified of your response and can edit what they wrote;
  • gives readers your side of the story and protects your online reputation.

Why a perfect score isn't the goal

A 2017 Northwestern University study (Spiegel Research Center) of online shopping found that purchase likelihood usually peaks at ratings between 4.0 and 4.7 stars and falls as ratings approach a perfect 5.0. A perfect score can look too good to be true.

Many consumers also read the owner's replies before they decide. Online ratings are digital word of mouth, and each reply is a small piece of marketing for your brand.

How to write a response to a negative review

An effective response follows a few simple steps, whether you're a plumber in Elche or run a restaurant in Alicante:

  1. Calm down and check the facts in your records to see what actually happened.
  2. Start by thanking the reviewer for their feedback, even when it stings.
  3. Acknowledge the issue and apologise if you made a mistake. Take responsibility for what's yours, not for what isn't.
  4. Explain briefly, without excuses. One or two sentences of context are enough.
  5. Offer a concrete way to resolve it, such as coming back to check the job, without making it conditional on an updated rating.
  6. Take it offline. Invite them to call or email you directly, and keep personal details out of your public reply.
  7. Keep it short and sign it with your name: it feels more genuine than a generic reply.

Once you've fixed the issue, check in a few days later to make sure they're happy, and let them know you're available if anything else comes up. A customer whose complaint is handled well can become even more loyal (what marketing calls the service recovery paradox), and may update their rating of their own accord. Never offer anything in exchange for an update.

Find the right tone

Write as you would speak to the person face to face: polite, simple and without jargon, in a voice that fits your brand. Leave out irony and capital letters, and keep in mind the future clients who will read your reply too.

If you're upset, take your time: it's best to write a draft and let it rest before you come back to it.

Example responses to negative reviews

Treat these responses as a starting point: adapt each one to the situation and replace the details in brackets.

When a job took longer than promised

A frequent complaint for plumbers and electricians in Valencia:

“Hello [name], thank you for taking the time to write. You're right: we arrived later than we said, and that isn't the service we want to offer. We've changed how we plan our visits so it doesn't happen again. Feel free to call us on [phone number]. Best regards, [your name]”

When the customer thinks you're too expensive

Explain what the price includes instead of arguing:

“Hello [name], thank you for your feedback. We understand that price matters, which is why our quotes detail what they include: materials, labour and travel. If you like, we can go through yours together: just write to [email]. Kind regards, [your name]”

When you got it wrong

If the customer is right, for example about a wrong order or a faulty product, say so plainly:

“Hello [name], thank you for letting us know, and we're sorry: [describe the mistake] shouldn't have happened. We've already put a solution in place. Write to us at [email] and we'll sort it out quickly. Thank you for the chance to do better. [Your name]”

When the rating has no comment

“Hello [name], we're sorry your experience wasn't what you expected. We'd love to understand what went wrong so we can put it right. Could you tell us more at [email]? Thank you, [your name]”

When you can't find the reviewer in your records

If you think it isn't from a real client, stay polite and factual:

“Hello, we're sorry to hear this, but we can't find any job or visit matching your details. Please contact us with more information so we can help. If this doesn't relate to a real experience with our business, we'll report it. [Your name]”

Mistakes to avoid when you reply

When you respond in a hurry, it's easy to turn a bad situation into a worse one and damage your reputation. Steer clear of:

  • replying in anger: a heated reply stays visible long after you've calmed down;
  • arguing, being sarcastic or blaming the client: readers judge your tone, not who was right;
  • revealing personal details such as a full name, an address or the product someone bought;
  • pasting the same generic text under every comment;
  • ignoring the complaint: if you simply stay silent, readers only see one side of the story;
  • negotiating compensation in public: if you consider a refund or a discount fair, offer to settle it privately;
  • offering a gift, a discount or money for changing or deleting a review: it breaks Google's fake engagement policy.

What to do about fake or abusive reviews

Not all negative reviews are fake, no matter how unfair you think they are. Only those that break Google's policies can be removed, such as:

  • spam, or content that isn't based on a real experience with your business;
  • offensive, hateful or harassing language;
  • conflicts of interest, for example a comment by a current or former employee or by a competing company;
  • private information, or content that isn't related to your business.

How to report a review

  1. Open your Business Profile and find the comment you want to flag.
  2. Choose the report option next to it and select the reason.
  3. Send it: the report is checked against the policies, which can take several days.

If it stays up, you can appeal the decision; meanwhile, reply politely so readers see your side.

If someone demands money to remove a review

Don't pay and don't negotiate. Demanding money, products or services for not posting a bad review, or for removing one, is a serious violation of Google's policies, and there's a specific form to report this kind of extortion.

Keep screenshots of the messages as evidence.

When should you reply?

There's no official deadline, but try to respond within the first 24 to 48 hours: it tells anyone reading that you're paying attention. Calm down first, though: a measured reply a day later is more likely to win the person back than an angry one.

If you have a team, decide who answers and make sure the team agrees on the tone. Check your listing once a week and turn on notifications so that no negative reviews go unanswered. The same approach works on Facebook, Tripadvisor or Yelp, and for comments on your social media.

Can AI help you write your responses?

Yes, as a starting point: AI tools such as ChatGPT can suggest a first draft or a better wording. Read every response before posting it, adapt it and don't paste clients' details into the tool. Our guide to AI tools for freelancers explains what else they can do.

Prevention: collect more positive reviews

One bad rating weighs less among plenty of recent, positive ones. Ask every customer to rate you right after the job, with a direct link or a QR code and without gifts or pressure. Our guide to getting more reviews explains the whole process.

Frequently asked questions

Can a negative Google review be deleted?
Only if it breaks Google's policies, for example because it's spam, offensive, written by someone with a conflict of interest or not based on a real experience. You can report it from your listing. A legitimate opinion from an unhappy customer stays up, but you can respond to it professionally.
How long do I have to reply to a negative review?
There's no official deadline. It's best to respond within the first 24 to 48 hours, once you've calmed down: a quick, measured reply shows that you care about your customers. Try not to leave any complaint unanswered.
Do negative reviews affect Google Maps ranking?
Google says that review count and review score are part of how it judges a business's prominence in local results: more reviews and positive ratings can improve local ranking. It doesn't say that replying improves your position: it says replying shows customers that you value their feedback.
Can I ask a customer to change their review?
Yes, tactfully, once you've genuinely solved the customer's problem. You can say: “We're glad we could sort this out. If you feel your experience has changed, you're welcome to update your review.” Never apply pressure or offer anything in exchange: that breaks the platform's rules.
How many positive reviews do I need to offset a bad one?
There's no fixed number: it depends on the ratings you receive. Ask every client to rate you, not only the happy ones, without gifts or discounts. The more genuine reviews you have, the less a single complaint weighs on your average.
Should I reply to positive reviews too?
It's worth it: a short thank-you that mentions the job can help build the relationship. You don't have to answer every one, but prioritise critical comments and those that ask a question.
