Your site is often the first place a new client sees your tattoos outside social media. Good tattoo studio website design shows off your portfolio, introduces the team, explains where the studio is and makes booking easy. Instagram is great for sharing fresh ink, but it doesn't belong to you: one change to the algorithm and your reach can drop overnight. This guide covers the features, design choices and pages your site needs, with tips for tattoo artists and studios in Valencia, Alicante, Elche and the rest of Spain.

Why do tattoo artists need their own website?

Because it's the one place online that you fully control. It brings your best pieces, your phone number and your address together, and it can appear in search results when someone looks for a tattoo artist in your town, even if they've never seen your profile.

Instagram is a showcase, not a home

Instagram is a great way to show new pieces and keep in touch with your followers, but you don't decide who sees your posts, and people who look for tattoos on a search engine don't always land on your profile. A website complements it: share fresh tattoo photos on your profile, and use your site to organise your best pieces, explain how you work and give people a clear way to reach you.

Your profile and your site, linked both ways

Put a link to your website in your bio, and a link to your profile in the site's footer. Every site we build includes a link to each of your profiles, so your online presence stays connected. To decide where to spend your posting effort, see which networks suit a small business.

Designed for phones first

Many of your clients will look you up on their mobile, often straight after seeing your latest piece. Your site should load fast, show large, sharp photos and keep the buttons to call or message you within easy reach (here's why speed matters for your ranking).

Must-have features for your tattoo site

You need a portfolio sorted by style, a page for each artist, a simple way to book, practical details about the studio and clear pages on hygiene, healing and legal texts. Plan these core features before you start thinking about colours and fonts:

  • Portfolio: galleries of your best tattoo work, organised by style.
  • Artist pages: one for each resident and guest artist, with their style and experience.
  • Services: custom pieces, flash, cover-ups and touch-ups, with what each one involves.
  • Booking: a booking button, WhatsApp and a contact form, always in view.
  • Practical details: address, map, opening hours and directions.
  • Process: the steps from first idea to finished tattoo.
  • Hygiene and aftercare: how you work and how to look after a new tattoo.
  • Legal texts: legal notice, privacy policy and cookie policy.

Our checklist of what every professional website should include covers the basics for any trade; the rest of this article focuses on what matters for tattoo artists.

A homepage that shows your best work first

When building your homepage, open with a few of your best tattoo photos, one sentence that says what you tattoo and where (for example, "Blackwork and fine line tattoos in Ruzafa, Valencia") and a button to book a consultation. Someone who arrives from your profile wants to confirm in seconds that this is the studio they saw, so keep the homepage design simple and let the work do the talking.

A page for each artist

Clients choose an artist as much as a studio, and they compare portfolios to do it. Give each artist their own page: a photo, a few lines about their background and experience, what is unique about their approach, a selection of their best tattoo pieces and a link to book with them. If you host guest artists from other studios, list their dates so their followers know when to come.

The process, from idea to custom design

Explain the steps so clients know what to expect:

  1. the consultation;
  2. the sketch;
  3. creating the final design;
  4. the tattoo session;
  5. the care afterwards.

People who know what comes next arrive with fewer doubts, and you answer the same questions less often.

The space and its atmosphere

Show the place itself: the front desk, the booths and the details that make it yours. A few photos and a short description help first-timers picture the experience and see that it's a clean, professional space before they walk in.

Web design tips: let your art set the style

Good web design for tattoo artists puts your art first: a clean layout, large photos, bold but readable type and simple navigation. The design should feel as unique as your studio, whether that's dark and dramatic or bright and minimal.

Dark or light backgrounds?

Dark or black backgrounds often make tattoo photos stand out, especially blackwork and strong colour pieces, but they aren't compulsory. A light, minimal design can suit fine line and delicate work. What matters is contrast: text that's easy to read and photos that look on screen the way they do on skin.

Typography, logo and brand

Your logo, colours and typography are part of your brand. An artist known for Japanese or American traditional tattoos can echo that style in the headings and graphics, as long as menus and body text stay readable. Avoid generic templates that could belong to any company: your site should have the same personality as your tattoos and reflect your unique artistry.

Simple navigation and an intuitive layout

Keep the menu short, for example Portfolio, Artists, About and Book. An intuitive interface means visitors never wonder where to tap next. Ensure the booking button stays in view wherever they are, not hidden at the bottom: that small design choice makes a real difference to the user experience.

Responsive design for every screen

A responsive design adapts the layout to phones, tablets and computers. On a phone, the navigation should fit behind one menu button, galleries should scroll smoothly and photos should open full screen. Before you share the link, test the responsive version on your own mobile and on a friend's to check the experience is smooth.

Big photos, fast pages

Tattoo photos are heavy files. Compress them before uploading to ensure they load quickly, and avoid video that plays automatically on the homepage: a stunning gallery can lose visitors if it takes too long to load.

Three layouts that suit tattoo websites

Look at great tattoo website designs and you'll often spot one of these three patterns:

  • The solo artist: a homepage with your strongest pieces in a full-width grid, a short bio and one booking button.
  • The multi-artist studio: a homepage that leads to each artist's page, each with a selection of pieces and a booking link.
  • The walk-in shop: opening hours, a map and the flash designs available that week, all on the first screen.

How to showcase your tattoo portfolio online

Show fewer, better pieces, with one gallery per style and each photo labelled with the style, the artist and whether the tattoo is fresh or healed. People looking for a tattoo want to see your art before they read anything, so make it easy to browse.

One gallery for each tattoo style

Start by creating a gallery for each style you want to keep tattooing, with a clear title; group related styles together if you only have a few pieces of each. Here's how you might approach each one:

StyleWhat to show
RealismWell-lit photos and a close-up of the shading
Traditional and old schoolThe full design and a close-up of the bold lines
Neo-traditionalThe whole piece and a close-up of the colour
Blackwork and dotworkThe contrast, without filters that change the black ink
Anime and colourColours true to the original design

Fresh and healed tattoos

If you can, add photos of healed tattoos as well as fresh ones: they show how your work holds up over time, not just on the day. Note on each photo whether the piece is fresh or healed and who did it, without personal details about the person wearing it.

Fewer pieces, better chosen

Professional portfolios aren't albums of everything you've tattooed. Choose the pieces that best represent your artistry and the work you want to keep doing, remove the ones that no longer represent you, and review the selection whenever you finish a piece you like more.

Photos, video and alt text

A few habits make your photos more useful:

  • photograph each tattoo with plenty of light, without filters that change the tones;
  • show the whole piece first, then a close-up;
  • add a short video of a healed piece moving with the body, which shows what a still photo misses;
  • give each image a short alt text: it helps people who use screen readers, and it helps search engines understand what the photo shows.

Real photos, with consent

If a person can be recognised in a photo, you need their consent: in Spain, this is covered by the law on the right to one's own image (derecho a la propia imagen). Ask for it in writing and keep the authorisation. The same care applies to personal data: use a client's phone number or email only for what they asked you for. Booking, deposits and consultations

Make getting in touch simple: a visible button that opens the booking tool you already use, a WhatsApp button and a contact form. Bookings and deposits are handled in your booking tool, not on the site itself.

A link to your booking tool

If you already take bookings online, your site only needs a "Book now" button that opens that tool, where each appointment is managed. WebAutonomos websites don't include a booking system: we add a button or a simple link to the tool you already use. Our guide to online booking tools explains how to choose one.

WhatsApp and contact forms

Put the WhatsApp button in the header so people can message you straight from their phone (see how to set up WhatsApp Business). Every website we build also includes a contact form that sends enquiries to your email.

What to ask in a tattoo enquiry

Enquiry forms save a round of messages. Ask people to provide:

  • their idea or design, in a few words;
  • where on the body they want it;
  • the approximate size;
  • whether they have reference images;
  • which artist they'd like, if they have a preference.

Then reply as soon as you can, while they're still deciding (a fast reply is part of customer care), with the next steps: the consultation, the design, the date and the conditions of the session.

Deposits and prices

If you want to take a deposit to confirm an appointment, your booking tool has to allow it: your website only sends clients to it.

On prices, rather than publishing fixed figures, explain what the cost depends on:

  • the size of the piece;
  • its placement on the body;
  • how detailed the design is.

Location, opening hours and clients from abroad

Before their first tattoo appointment, people want to know where you are and when you're open:

  • full address and a map;
  • opening days and hours, and whether you're offering walk-ins;
  • phone and email;
  • how to get there from the centre and where to park.

Details that match your business listing

Keep your address and opening hours identical to those on your business listing, and update both when they change (here's why that consistency matters).

Clients who travel for a tattoo

If clients travel from Madrid, Barcelona or abroad to be tattooed by one of your artists, explain how to get there and how to plan the session. Hygiene and aftercare information

Explaining how you work builds trust. Present it as general guidance that doesn't replace any medical advice.

How you work

Describe, in your own words and only what you really do, how you clean and prepare the booth and the equipment before each tattoo. Avoid promises: don't advertise health outcomes or replace a doctor's advice. Check what your autonomous community's health regulations require before you publish anything.

Looking after a new tattoo

You can publish general advice on caring for the skin after the session, making clear that it's guidance only. For any health question, send clients to a doctor.

WordPress, a template or a custom design: what's right for you?

There are three main ways to get online: building the site yourself with WordPress or a website builder, paying a freelance web designer or an agency for a custom design, or letting someone do it for you. These options cost different amounts of money and effort, and offer different features.

WordPress with a tattoo theme

WordPress is open-source software that you install on your own hosting. Many ready-made WordPress themes are built for tattooists, with galleries, artist pages and space for a booking button, but themes vary a lot: choose a theme that is responsive and keeps the booking button visible, and check that the theme is still updated by its developer. The catch is maintenance: WordPress, the theme and its plugins (add-on tools that bring extra features) need regular updates to stay secure, and when something breaks, you are your own support team. If you enjoy the technical side, it offers plenty of flexibility; if you don't, it eats into your tattooing time.

Website builders and templates

Builders such as Wix or Squarespace give you drag-and-drop templates and a simple interface, including hosting and support, for a subscription fee. They're easy to start with, but a popular template can look like many other sites, the design options have limits, and you still have to write the texts, prepare the photos and keep the content up to date. If you're comparing the two approaches, read Wix or WordPress.

A custom design from a freelancer or a web design company

A freelance web designer or a web design company can create a fully custom site built around your brand, from the colour palette to the layout of each section. In Spain, a professional website costs around €800 to €2,000 from a freelancer and €1,500 to €4,000 from an agency, before maintenance (Social Media Pymes, 2026).

Common mistakes on tattoo websites

These mistakes cost you enquiries, and all of them can be fixed:

  • photos with filters that change the tones of the tattoos;
  • a portfolio with no order and no indication of who did each piece;
  • no clear way to contact you or book;
  • publishing a recognisable person without their consent;
  • an address and opening hours that don't match your listing;
  • huge images that make pages slow on a phone;
  • a gallery with no fresh content for months.
