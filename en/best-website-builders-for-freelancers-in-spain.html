Our criteria

We compared each option on four criteria: total cost over two years, the time you'd need to put in yourself, whether an English interface is available, and whether the Spanish legal texts are covered by default.

What we left out

We didn't test page load speed or run a security audit of each platform, so this comparison doesn't rank them on either.

Best free option: Webnode or Jimdo

Both have a genuinely free plan with no time limit, not a free trial that ends after a set period.

What you get for free

Jimdo is the quickest to start with: answer a few questions about your business and its AI helps you create a first draft to adjust in the visual editor. Webnode lets you add a second language later, though multi-language content only starts with its paid Standard plan, not the free one.

Where it falls short

The limit is the same on both: you work on a subdomain such as yourbusiness.jimdosite.com, not your own domain name, and the space for photos and pages is small. It is fine for testing an idea before you commit to anything; it is not built to compete with other businesses on Google, because a subdomain looks less professional and is harder to rank.

  • Good for: trying out an idea, a hobby project, or a placeholder while you decide on a domain name
  • Not ideal for: a business that wants to be found on Google under its own name

Best paid builder: Wix Core

Wix is one of the most complete website builders on the market: hundreds of templates organised by business type, an app store for extra features, and its own AI tools if you want a first draft generated for you. Core, the plan we picked for a small business, costs €20 monthly if billed annually, or €25 if billed monthly, with the domain name free for the first year. It suits freelancers who want creative control over their website's design, with e-commerce add-ons if you want to sell online too.

What's included

  • A drag-and-drop editor and a blog tool
  • Basic on-page SEO settings and a mobile app to edit content on the go
  • Email marketing tools and social media links built in
  • Hosting, an SSL certificate and customer support included in the plan

The time you need to put in

In exchange for that flexibility, you build the website yourself. Allow ten to thirty hours if you have never used a builder before: choosing a template, writing your own content, adding your services and checking it on mobile. The result depends on your eye for design and on how well you write, not on the tool.

  • Good for: someone who wants full control over the design and is happy to learn the editor
  • Not ideal for: someone who would rather not spend evenings building a website

Best price with hosting: Hostinger Premium

On paper it is unbeatable: €2.59 a month for a builder with hosting included.

The small print

That headline rate requires paying four years upfront in one go, and it doesn't stay that low afterwards (see the exact figures under the comparison table above). It's still the cheapest option in euros once you accept that commitment, but it is not a cheap plan you can cancel whenever you like.

What you're signing up for

Hostinger's editor is simpler than Wix's, with fewer templates and fewer design options, but it covers the basics: a contact form, an SSL certificate and basic SEO settings. You build the website yourself, which means fifteen to forty hours of work if you start from nothing, and you stay responsible for keeping the content up to date afterwards.

  • Good for: someone comfortable building a site themselves who wants the lowest euro cost
  • Not ideal for: someone who wants a finished site without the setup work

What about WordPress or Squarespace?

Both come up constantly in "best website builder" articles, and neither is in the table above, for different reasons.

WordPress.org

WordPress.org is free software, not a hosted builder: you still need to buy hosting separately, choose a theme, and install and update your own plugins for a contact form, SEO and security. It gives you full control and a very wide choice of themes, but it is also the only option here where the technical upkeep is yours: someone has to keep WordPress, the theme and every plugin updated, or the site becomes a security risk. Some freelancers hire developers for the initial WordPress setup and then edit the content themselves: a good compromise, as long as you agree beforehand on who keeps everything updated after launch.

  • Good for: someone who already knows WordPress and wants full control over every detail
  • Not ideal for: a freelancer who doesn't want an extra piece of software to maintain

Squarespace

Squarespace is a paid builder in the same category as Wix, with polished templates aimed at portfolios, photographers and online stores. If you're a freelancer whose work is visual and portfolio-led, it is worth a look; if you mainly need opening hours, a list of services and a contact form, it offers more design than you will use.

  • Good for: photographers, designers and other visual work where the portfolio does the selling
  • Not ideal for: a tradesperson or clinic that only needs the basics

What about hiring a freelance web designer?

A freelance web designer builds the website for you, like an agency, but works alone: the same person handles the design, the layout of your content and the technical setup. In Spain, a professional website from a freelance designer costs €800 to €2,000, not counting maintenance (Social Media Pymes, August 2026). Freelance designers vary widely in speciality and style, and the ones with a strong portfolio aren't always available at short notice, so it's worth comparing a few before settling on one.

How to find the right designer

  • Look at their portfolio: websites they have built for businesses like yours, and how those websites look on a phone
  • Ask who hosts the website, who pays for the domain and who keeps everything updated once it is live
  • Ask how a change is charged after launch, and how quickly they usually reply
  • Check that the domain name will be registered in your own name, not the designer's
  • Search freelance platforms or listing sites, or ask other freelancers you know for a recommendation
  • Good for: a business that wants a custom design and one person to deal with from start to finish
  • Not ideal for: a tight budget, or a business that needs its website live quickly

Best for a custom project: a Spanish design agency

If you need an online shop with thousands of products, an integration with your management software or a full brand design, none of the builders above will do, and it goes beyond what the €15 monthly and €349 plans include. That is where an agency makes sense: typically €1,000 to €6,000 upfront, plus €50 to €200 monthly for maintenance afterwards.

What this option does that a builder can't

An agency's designers and developers can build custom features from scratch: a booking system tied to your own database, a product catalogue with thousands of items, or a brand identity designed around your business rather than a template. It is not expensive for the sake of it; it is a different kind of job, with meetings, a brief and a build measured in weeks rather than a day.

  • Good for: an online shop with a large catalogue, or software integrations a template can't handle
  • Not ideal for: a straightforward business site, where the extra cost buys little you'd notice

Website builder or done-for-you service: how to decide

Price alone doesn't answer the question, because the cheapest builders only look cheap if your own time is free.

Choose a builder if

  • You enjoy design and writing, or want to learn how to create a website yourself
  • You have ten to forty hours to spend on it over the next few weeks
  • Your needs are simple: a few pages, with no legal complexity beyond the basics
  • You're happy to find answers in a help centre on your own when something doesn't work

Choose a done-for-you service if

  • Your time is worth more to your business than the money you'd save doing it yourself
  • You want the Spanish legal pages written for you, not a blank template
  • You would rather see a finished demo than start from an empty page

How to create a website with a builder: five steps to get started

If you decide to do it yourself, the steps are much the same on Wix, Hostinger, Jimdo or Webnode. Doing them in the right order will help you finish sooner, instead of leaving a half-built website online.

  • Gather your content before you open the editor: what you do, the area you cover, your opening hours, your contact details and a few good photos of your work
  • Pick a template close to your trade, then change the text and photos rather than the layout
  • Add the Spanish legal texts before you publish: the section further down explains which ones you need
  • Test the contact form yourself: send a request and check that it arrives in the email inbox you actually read
  • Connect your own domain name, check the website on your phone, then share the link on your Google Business Profile so people can find it

What features actually matter for a freelancer's website

Templates and AI page generators get a lot of the marketing attention, but for a freelancer or a small business, the features that decide whether a website actually brings in clients are more ordinary. Before you compare feature lists, it helps to know what you're looking for: a way for people to find you when they search, and an easy way for them to get in touch once they do.

Must-have features

  • A contact form and a visible phone number or WhatsApp button, so a client doesn't have to search for how to reach you
  • A mobile-friendly design: many of your clients will look you up on their phone, not a desktop
  • An SSL certificate (the padlock in the browser): Wix, Hostinger and WebAutonomos include one, so check that any other option does too
  • Your own domain name, not a subdomain, if you want to be found on Google
  • Easy updates: check how easy it is to navigate the editor and change your opening hours or a photo yourself, or how quickly someone else does it for you
  • A website that loads fast on a phone: a slow one can make people give up before they contact you (why speed also affects your Google ranking)
  • Text and headings a search engine can read for basic SEO, not just images, so people looking for what you offer can actually find you
  • Contact details and opening hours that match your Google Business Profile, so Google shows consistent information about your business

Nice-to-have features

  • A blog, if you plan to write about your work: it helps with SEO but takes ongoing time
  • Email marketing tools, useful once you have a list of past clients to write to
  • A booking or payment integration, only worth it if you take appointments or sell online
  • Support you can reach if you get stuck: a help centre, a forum or a person to contact

Other features to compare

Beyond the basics, check which of these each plan includes before you commit: an app store for extra features, and e-commerce tools if you plan to sell online. Compare the plan you would pay for, not the free tier, since a free plan doesn't always include them.

Common mistakes freelancers make when choosing a website builder

These mistakes often come from comparing the sticker price alone, without factoring in what happens after you sign up.

  • Picking the cheapest plan and then facing a renewal price that's much higher after the first term
  • Building on a subdomain and only later finding out it struggles to rank on Google
  • Skipping the legal pages a Spanish-registered business needs, whatever the site's language
  • Choosing a provider with no English support, then struggling with the back-and-forth
  • Assuming a template will look finished without writing real content for it
  • Leaving out what clients are looking for, such as the area you cover or a clear way to contact you, which can send them back to Google to pick someone else

Do you need a website if you already have a Google Business Profile?

A Google Business Profile shows your hours, reviews and location on Maps, but it doesn't replace a website. Only a website gives you these:

  • A page for each service, with enough detail to explain what makes you different
  • Your own domain name, rather than a profile hosted on Google's own platform
  • A layout and content you control, instead of a fixed template of fields

The two work together rather than substitute for each other: a client can find you on Google Maps, then open your website to see your services in detail before getting in touch.

What if you already have a website and want to switch?

Moving to a new builder or provider doesn't mean losing your domain name, as long as it's registered in your own name rather than your current provider's. Point the domain to the new website once it's ready, keep the old one live until the new one is, and redirect the old page addresses if the URLs change, so you don't lose whatever ranking you already have.

What to check before you switch

  • Who the domain name is registered to, and whether you can transfer it or just repoint it
  • Whether your current provider lets you export your content, or whether you'll rewrite it from scratch
  • Whether the old page addresses will redirect to the new ones, so search engines don't start from zero

Three things English speakers in Spain should check

The Spanish legal pages

If your business is established in Spain, your website must show a legal notice (aviso legal), a privacy policy and a cookie policy, whatever language it is in. A builder gives you the tools; the text with your NIF or NIE, your address and your registration details is still yours to write. What they must contain.

The language you get help in

Wix, Hostinger, Jimdo and Webnode all have an English interface. A Spanish web design agency may not work in English, and a website project means a lot of back-and-forth; check this before you commit to months of emails. If the interface itself is in English, check whether the help articles are too, since translated menus don't always come with translated support pages.

A second language

Many clients in Spain will search for you in Spanish. If you need the site in two languages, check which plan includes it before you start: on Webnode, for example, multi-language only starts with its Standard plan. At WebAutonomos, a site in up to four languages costs nothing extra.

A note on Weebly

Square announced in June 2026 that it is closing Weebly in 67 countries, where sites are taken offline on 27 September 2026. Spain and the United Kingdom are not on the list, so Weebly sites in Spain keep working (Weebly's announcement). It is still a reminder of a real risk: if a platform closes or changes its terms, your website goes with it. With the domain name registered in your own name, you at least keep your address and can point it to a new website.

How to choose in two questions

First: do you have ten to forty hours to spend on it? If you do, a builder is cheaper. If you don't, the builder's price is misleading, because the time you don't have also costs money. Second: do you want to be able to change provider without starting again? Then make sure the domain name is registered in your own name, whichever option you choose.
